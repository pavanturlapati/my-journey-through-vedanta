# Day 7 of Navaratri — Kalaratri: What Am I Afraid to Look At?

*Part of the [Navaratri series](navaratri.md).*

> **A guided reflection for the seventh day of Navaratri**  
> Explore how Kalaratri is worshipped, the traditional accounts of her fierce form, the meaning of her symbols, and a Vedantic reading of darkness as ignorance that light can remove. The questions are invitations, not tests.

## 1. How the day is observed

Kalaratri (*Kālarātri*) is worshipped as the seventh of the nine forms of Goddess Durga, the Navadurga, on the seventh day of Navaratri. Her name is commonly explained as "the night of time" or "the dark night": *kāla* can mean both time and black, and *rātri* means night. She is the most fierce-looking of the nine forms, and yet she is also known by the name *Shubhankari*, "the one who does good."

Customs differ widely, so what follows is a general picture, not a rule:

- **Temple visits and home worship.** Many families pray at home, or visit a temple of the Goddess if it is feasible.
- **Fasting.** Fasting during Navaratri is common, though its form varies by family and region.
- **Night-time worship.** Because of her name, some traditions place special weight on worship in the night hours of this day. In Bengal, for example, the seventh day, *Saptami*, is the day the festival's main rituals begin in many communities.
- **A common mantra:**

  > ॐ देवी कालरात्र्यै नमः  
  > *Om Devi Kalaratryai Namah*  
  > "Om, salutations to the Goddess Kalaratri."

- **Regional flavours of Navaratri** (festival-wide, not specific to Day 7):
    - In **Gujarat**, *Garba* and *Dandiya Raas* are danced in the evenings through the nine nights.
    - In **West Bengal**, the festival is celebrated as *Durga Puja*, with elaborate community pandals, mostly in the last days.
    - In **Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh**, many homes display *Golu* or *Bommala Koluvu*, a tiered arrangement of dolls.
    - In **Karnataka**, Mysuru's *Dasara* is known for its processions and palace celebrations.

I do not have a fixed family ritual for this day beyond going to the temple when possible. If your family follows a particular practice, that practice comes first. This article is an additional way to reflect, not a replacement.

## 2. The story: the dark form that ends the darkness

Kalaratri is often connected, in popular tellings, with the account in the *Devi Mahatmya* of the demon Raktabija. He had a boon that every drop of his blood falling to the ground would produce another demon like him, so wounding him only multiplied the enemy. In this telling the Goddess, in a fierce dark form, drank his blood before it could touch the ground, and so ended the threat. In the *Devi Mahatmya* itself this form is named Kali or Chamunda, and Kalaratri is commonly treated as the same or a closely related form. Traditions differ on this.

A second thread is that the Goddess in this form is said to be the one who ends fear for her devotees. Devotional accounts describe that ghosts, fear and evil influences flee at the mere mention of her name.

Versions and emphasis vary between texts, lineages and regions, so please read this as one common telling.

## 3. The form and its symbols

In the traditional description, Kalaratri has a dark complexion, loose and untied hair, three eyes and four arms. She rides a donkey, and she is described as wearing a necklace that shines like lightning. **The tradition gives the form; the interpretations below are suggestions** and may differ between lineages. Published descriptions are not fully uniform, so details such as which hand holds which object vary.

| Feature | Traditional description | A possible meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Dark complexion** | Described as dark as night. | The unknown, and what we cannot see in ourselves; also the stillness of night. |
| **Loose, flowing hair** | Dishevelled and untied. | Freedom from convention, and the wildness of what we usually keep tidy. |
| **Three eyes** | Round, described as shining like the universe. | Sight that goes beyond the ordinary: past, present and future, or knowledge beyond the senses. |
| **Lightning-like necklace** | Her necklace is described as flashing. | A sudden flash of insight in the dark. |
| **Raised hand (abhaya mudra)** | One right hand is shown in a gesture of protection. | "Do not be afraid", from the very form that looks most frightening. |
| **Boon-giving hand (varada mudra)** | Another right hand grants blessings. | Her fierceness is on the side of the devotee. |
| **Iron hook and sword** | Commonly described in the left hands. | Cutting through what binds and misleads. |
| **Donkey as mount** | A donkey is described as her vehicle. | Humility and endurance. It is an unexpected mount for a fierce goddess, and it is sometimes read as a reminder that her power does not depend on show. |

The contrast is the point of the image: the most terrifying form carries the gestures of protection and blessing. The tradition says that her appearance is fearful, but her nature is auspicious.

Compare her with Day 6. Katyayani stands up against an outer opponent with a sword and a lotus. Kalaratri is closer to the dark that I would rather not look at.

## 4. Everyday example: the unopened envelope

Imagine a person who has a letter on the table, perhaps a medical result, a bill, or a message they know they should answer. They keep walking past it. Every day it sits there, the worry grows bigger than whatever is inside it.

Finally, they open it. The news is not good, but not as bad as the picture they had been carrying in their mind. And now there is something to do. **The fear of the thing was often heavier than the thing itself.**

Ask yourself:

- What am I avoiding, a conversation, a decision, a memory, or a truth about myself?
- What picture of it have I built in my mind, and how much of that picture is evidence?
- Who could sit with me while I look at it?

## 5. A Vedantic lens: from darkness to light

A well-known prayer from the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (1.3.28) says:

> असतो मा सद्गमय । तमसो मा ज्योतिर्गमय । मृत्योर्मा अमृतं गमय ॥

"Lead me from the unreal to the real, from darkness to light, from death to immortality."

In Vedanta, darkness is a common picture for *ajñāna*, ignorance: not a thing that has to be fought, but an absence that disappears when light is brought in. A dark room is not removed by hitting it with a stick, but by lighting a lamp.

Read this way, Kalaratri's image suggests two threads:

1. **Facing the dark is the first step to light.** The Goddess is shown as the night itself, and yet she is also the one who ends fear. Looking directly at what I fear, with a steady mind, can be where understanding starts. See [Vedanta Must Be Practiced](../chapter-01/07-practice-vedanta.md).
2. **What frightens me may be smaller than the story I tell about it.** Vedanta often says that fear arises from a mistaken idea, such as taking a rope for a snake. The classical picture is that the snake disappears when the light shows the rope. This is a traditional illustration, and I use it here only as an image. See [Desire, Self and Worldly Dependence](../chapter-01/10-desire-self-and-worldly-dependence.md).

This is a **reflective reading**, not a claim that every tradition interprets the Goddess in this way. The deeper Vedantic point is understood through careful study with a qualified teacher and not through a single verse or an image. If you are dealing with serious fear, anxiety or distress, please also speak with a qualified professional. These reflections are not a substitute.

## 6. Questions for reflection

Set aside five to ten quiet minutes. You may look at an image of Kalaratri, offer a prayer, or simply sit. Write a few words for each prompt. "I don't know yet" is a valid answer.

1. **What am I afraid to look at?** It might be a decision, a conversation, a loss, or something about myself.
2. **What story have I built around it?** How much of what I imagine is known, and how much is guessed?
3. **What has fear cost me?** What have I avoided, delayed or not said because of it?
4. **What helps me stay steady in the dark?** A prayer, a person, a breath, a routine?
5. **What is one small light I can switch on this week?** One step toward looking at it, even for a minute.

You do not have to answer all five in one sitting. Choose one that feels alive and return to the others later.

## 7. The invitation of Day 7

Kalaratri's image holds two things together that we often keep apart: the fearful and the protective. The form is dark, the hair is wild, and yet one hand says "do not be afraid."

Religious worship of the Goddess remains meaningful in its own right. This reflection is one additional way to engage with the day: to notice what I avoid, and to see whether a little light would change how it looks.

**You do not have to face everything tonight.** Naming one thing you have been avoiding, and looking at it gently for a minute, is a sincere place to begin.
