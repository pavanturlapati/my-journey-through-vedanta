# Navaratri: Nine Nights, Two Ways of Understanding

*Part of the [Navaratri series](navaratri.md).*

> A personal study reflection exploring Navaratri through two complementary lenses: the religious traditions of Hinduism and the inward inquiry encouraged by Vedanta.

Navaratri is a beautiful subject to explore through Vedanta because it offers two ways of understanding the same festival: **what we worship outwardly and what we can learn inwardly**.

This article is a learning aid, not a definitive account of every Hindu tradition. Customs and interpretations vary by region, lineage, and family. The Vedantic reflections below are offered as a way to contemplate the festival; they should not be confused with a single universally prescribed scriptural interpretation.

## 1. What is Navaratri?

The Sanskrit word *Navaratri* combines *nava*, meaning “nine,” and *ratri*, meaning “night.” Navaratri is a Hindu festival celebrating the Divine Feminine. In many traditions, worship focuses on Goddess Durga and her forms; other observances also emphasize Lakshmi and Saraswati.

The festival is celebrated in different ways across India and by Hindu communities around the world. The autumn Navaratri is especially widely observed, although other Navaratri observances occur during the year.

There is a religious explanation involving the Goddess and her victory over evil. There is also a philosophical way of looking at the festival: the nine nights can become an opportunity for inner reflection, discipline, and spiritual growth.

# Part I — Navaratri through Vedanta

## 2. Begin with a central idea of Vedanta

In Advaita Vedanta, our essential nature is the *Atman*, which is not separate from the ultimate reality, *Brahman*. Yet in everyday life, we tend to identify ourselves with our body, emotions, thoughts, desires, achievements, and intellect.

We may say:

- “I am angry.”
- “I am a failure because I made a mistake.”
- “I am happy because I achieved something.”
- “I am important because of my position.”

Vedanta invites us to investigate whether these changing experiences fully define who we are. The body changes. Emotions change. Thoughts change. Our professional roles and circumstances change.

**Vedanta asks us to discover the deeper reality of the Self rather than mistake the changing personality for our complete identity.**

This does not mean the body, mind, or worldly responsibilities are unimportant. It means learning to understand and use them without becoming completely identified with them.

## 3. Three groups of three days

One common devotional interpretation divides the nine days into three groups: Durga, Lakshmi, and Saraswati. This is a useful framework for reflection, though it is not the only way Navaratri is observed or interpreted.

### Days 1–3: Durga — courage and overcoming obstacles

Durga is worshipped as a powerful and protective form of the Divine Mother. As an inward reflection, these days can invite us to confront habits and impulses that hold us back: fear, uncontrolled reactions, laziness, resentment, or confusion.

The point is not simply to pray for problems to disappear. It is also to develop the courage and discipline to address them.

**Reflection:** *What within me is preventing me from becoming the person I want to be?*

### Days 4–6: Lakshmi — well-being and constructive effort

Lakshmi is associated with prosperity, auspiciousness, abundance, and well-being. Prosperity need not be understood as money alone; a well-lived life also depends on health, supportive relationships, competence, generosity, and wise use of one's abilities.

After beginning to overcome obstacles, we must cultivate positive qualities and use our capabilities constructively.

**Reflection:** *Am I using my abilities, time, and opportunities wisely?*

### Days 7–9: Saraswati — knowledge and discernment

Saraswati is associated with learning, the arts, wisdom, and knowledge. Through a Vedantic lens, these days can remind us to investigate what is lasting and what is temporary, and to seek understanding rather than settle for information alone.

We may become disciplined, successful, and knowledgeable about our work, yet still ask: *Do I understand myself, or do I simply know how to succeed in the world?*

**Reflection:** *Beyond my achievements and possessions, who am I?*

These three stages form a meaningful framework for spiritual reflection. They do not mean that observing nine days automatically produces spiritual realization.

## 4. A practical example

Imagine that you want to accomplish something meaningful in life, but you are distracted, impatient, and inconsistent.

**Stage 1 — Durga: Recognize and face your obstacles.** You notice that procrastination, emotional reactions, or constant distractions are getting in the way. You begin to address them with courage and discipline.

**Stage 2 — Lakshmi: Develop your potential.** As you become more consistent, you cultivate positive habits, competence, patience, relationships, and responsibility. Removing negative habits is not enough; you must also nurture positive qualities.

**Stage 3 — Saraswati: Seek deeper understanding.** You may now be disciplined and successful, but you investigate a deeper question: beyond your role and achievements, what is the nature of the “I” that experiences them?

The goal is not to become perfect in nine days. It is to become more conscious of how you think, act, and understand yourself.

## 5. How does this connect to the goal of Vedanta?

The ultimate goal of Advaita Vedanta is not merely to become a better, more disciplined, or more successful person. It is to gain liberating knowledge of one's true nature as Atman, which is Brahman.

Discipline and positive qualities can support that journey, but they are not substitutes for self-knowledge.

- **Durga** can remind us of the strength to face obstacles.
- **Lakshmi** can remind us to cultivate a well-ordered, constructive life.
- **Saraswati** can remind us of the importance of knowledge and discernment.

One important distinction: these goddesses are not merely psychological qualities in Hindu religious traditions. They are worshipped as divine forms in their own right. A Vedantic interpretation can draw an inward lesson from that worship without denying its religious significance.

# Part II — Navaratri in religious tradition

## 6. Why do Hindus celebrate Navaratri?

In Hindu religious tradition, Navaratri celebrates the power, protection, and victory of the Divine Mother.

One of the best-known stories is the battle between Goddess Durga and the buffalo demon Mahishasura. Mahishasura had become a formidable force, threatening the gods and disturbing cosmic order. The combined divine powers of the gods manifested as the Goddess, Durga, who fought and defeated him.

Her victory represents the triumph of divine power and *dharma* — righteousness and the order that sustains life — over destructive forces.

Another well-known association, especially in parts of northern India, connects the season with Lord Rama's victory over Ravana, celebrated at Vijayadashami or Dussehra. These traditions share a theme of victory over evil, but they are not the same story.

## 7. The nine forms of Durga — Navadurga

In a widely followed tradition, each day honours one form of Goddess Durga. Collectively, these forms are called the *Navadurga*. The names and associations below summarize a common tradition; observances may vary.

### Day 1 — Shailaputri
*Daughter of the Mountain.* She is associated with the beginning of the devotional journey and with steadfastness.

### Day 2 — Brahmacharini
*Devotion and austerity.* She embodies penance, perseverance, self-restraint, and dedication to a spiritual path.

### Day 3 — Chandraghanta
*Courage and readiness.* Her form evokes courage and protection against harmful forces.

### Day 4 — Kushmanda
*Creative cosmic energy.* She is associated with the creation and radiance of the universe.

### Day 5 — Skandamata
*Motherhood and compassion.* As the mother of Skanda, she is associated with maternal love, care, and protection.

### Day 6 — Katyayani
*Strength and righteous action.* She is associated with overcoming evil and restoring righteousness.

### Day 7 — Kalaratri
*Fearlessness and protection.* Her fierce appearance represents the power that confronts and destroys terrifying or destructive forces.

### Day 8 — Mahagauri
*Purity and peace.* She is associated with purity, serenity, and spiritual renewal.

### Day 9 — Siddhidatri
*Spiritual attainments and fulfilment.* She is associated with *siddhis*, or extraordinary spiritual attainments, and the bestowal of perfections.

## 8. What happens during the nine days?

Religious observances may include prayer, fasting, temple visits, chanting, offerings, music, dance, and family gatherings. Customs vary widely by region and family.

- **Puja and prayer:** Many families establish a sacred space, light a lamp, and worship the Goddess. Some perform *kalash sthapana*, installing a ritual pot at the beginning of the observance.
- **Garba and Dandiya:** In Gujarat and many communities elsewhere, people gather for devotional and celebratory dances.

  ![Garba folk dance during Navaratri](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Garba_Folk_Dance.jpg?width=1000)

  *Image: Garba folk dance. [Image source and license information](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Garba_Folk_Dance.jpg).*
- **Durga Puja:** In West Bengal and other regions, the season features elaborate worship of Durga, artistic displays, and community celebrations.
- **Golu:** In parts of South India, families arrange dolls on tiered displays, invite visitors, and celebrate through music, devotion, and shared food.

  ![Golu doll display for Navaratri](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/7_steps_Golu_in_Melbourne_Australia_2023.jpg?width=1000)

  *Image: Golu doll display. [Image source and license information](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Navaratri).*
- **Fasting and offerings:** Some devotees observe dietary restrictions or fasts; practices depend on personal, family, and regional tradition.

Not every Hindu family follows the same rituals. Some observe strict fasting, others pray daily without fasting, and others primarily participate in cultural and community celebrations.

## 9. Day 10 — Vijayadashami

The festival culminates in Vijayadashami, also known as Dussehra in many places. Depending on the tradition, it may commemorate Durga's victory over Mahishasura, Rama's victory over Ravana, or the auspicious beginning of a new learning or professional endeavour.

For personal reflection, the tenth day can be a time to ask: *What have I learned, and what will I carry forward into daily life?*

# Part III — Bringing the perspectives together

## 10. Nine days of personal reflection

The following is a suggested reflection exercise inspired by the nine forms. It is **not** a claim that Hindu scriptures prescribe exactly these nine psychological lessons.

| Day | Form | A question for reflection |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Shailaputri | What is the foundation of my life? |
| 2 | Brahmacharini | Can I remain committed when something is difficult? |
| 3 | Chandraghanta | Do I face challenges courageously? |
| 4 | Kushmanda | What can I create or contribute to the world? |
| 5 | Skandamata | Am I caring toward others? |
| 6 | Katyayani | Do I act decisively when action is necessary? |
| 7 | Kalaratri | What fears or harmful habits must I confront? |
| 8 | Mahagauri | Can I bring greater clarity and peace into my life? |
| 9 | Siddhidatri | What does genuine fulfilment mean to me? |

You might spend a few quiet minutes each day reading about the form of the Goddess, observing your own thoughts and behaviour, and writing down a response. On Vijayadashami, choose one practical change to carry forward.

## 11. Religion and Vedanta: different, but potentially complementary

### The religious perspective

Goddess Durga is worshipped as the Divine Mother. Her forms represent divine power, protection, compassion, and victory over evil. Devotees express faith through prayer, offerings, fasting, and celebration.

### The Vedantic perspective

The festival can also inspire us to investigate our own nature, examine limiting attachments, cultivate a prepared mind, and seek knowledge of the Self.

### How they can complement each other

Worship can be an expression of devotion and reverence. Reflection can help us examine our lives. Vedantic inquiry goes further by asking whether we understand the true nature of the one who worships, thinks, acts, and experiences life.

These perspectives need not be in conflict. Many Hindus approach Navaratri through devotion, philosophical inquiry, or both. Vedanta encompasses multiple traditions, and not every school interprets the Goddess or the festival in exactly the same way.

## 12. Three questions to carry through Navaratri

For each of the nine days, consider three questions:

1. **Religious understanding:** What does this form of the Goddess represent in the tradition?
2. **Practical understanding:** What can I learn from that representation and apply in daily life?
3. **Vedantic inquiry:** What does this teach me about my mind, my attachments, and ultimately the nature of the Self?

The third question is the deepest. It moves beyond asking how to improve the person we believe ourselves to be and begins asking what we truly are.

That is where Navaratri can become more than a festival on the calendar: it can become an opportunity to combine devotion with self-inquiry.
