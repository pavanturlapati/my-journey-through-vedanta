# Day 1 of Navaratri — Shailaputri: What Is the Foundation of My Life?

> **A guided reflection for the first day of Navaratri**  
> Explore the traditional story of Shailaputri, the Daughter of the Mountain, and use its imagery as a starting point for personal inquiry. The questions are invitations, not tests. You do not need to reach a final answer today.

## 1. Who is Shailaputri?

Shailaputri is worshipped as the first of the nine forms of Goddess Durga, the Navadurga, during a widely followed Navaratri tradition. Her name comes from two Sanskrit words: *shaila*, meaning mountain, and *putri*, meaning daughter. She is known as the **Daughter of the Mountain** and is identified with Parvati, daughter of Himavan, the personification of the Himalayas.

In traditional images, Shailaputri is often shown riding a bull and holding a trident and a lotus. Devotees worship her as a divine form in her own right. Her story and imagery belong first to Hindu religious tradition; the personal reflections in this article are an additional way to contemplate the day, not a replacement for devotion or a claim that every symbol has one universally accepted meaning.

## 2. Why begin with the Daughter of the Mountain?

A mountain gives us a simple image to contemplate. Weather changes around it. Clouds come and go. There may be sunshine one day and a storm the next. The mountain remains grounded.

This is a **reflection inspired by the image of the mountain**, rather than a claim that this is the only religious meaning of Shailaputri.

The first day of a journey is a natural time to ask: *What am I building my life upon?* Before we decide where we want to go, it helps to notice what currently supports us—and what we rely on when life becomes difficult.

Think of a house. We may admire its rooms, furniture, and appearance, but the foundation is what supports the structure. In a similar way, our job, income, achievements, possessions, and social roles are visible parts of life. Beneath them are the values, habits, relationships, assumptions, and understanding that influence how we live.

The reflection is not asking you to reject work, family, achievement, or material security. It asks you to notice whether these are the whole foundation—or only parts of the house built upon it.

## 3. Everyday example: when the weather changes

Imagine two people who experience a setback at work.

One thinks, “I have lost this opportunity, so perhaps I am no longer capable or valuable.” The setback becomes a judgement about their entire self.

The other is disappointed too, but thinks, “This matters, and I need to learn from it. My role may change, but I can still act with integrity, learn, ask for help, and begin again.”

Neither person is free from pain. The difference is what each person leans on when circumstances change.

Now apply the same question to your own life:

- If your job title changed, what would still matter to you?
- If you made a mistake, what would help you recover without defining yourself by that mistake?
- If you did not know the answer to a problem, would you still feel you had something valuable to offer?
- What qualities would you hope to preserve through success and disappointment alike?

Do not choose answers because they sound spiritual. Notice what is actually true for you today.

## 4. What might “foundation” mean for an individual?

There is no single answer that everyone must adopt. A person's current foundation might include family, faith, service, learning, integrity, creativity, community, or a commitment to keep growing. Several may be important at once.

It can help to distinguish three layers:

### What I do

This includes work, caregiving, problem-solving, teaching, creating, and the roles we play. These can change throughout life.

### The qualities I cultivate

These might include honesty, courage, patience, generosity, curiosity, and the willingness to learn again. They influence how we perform our roles, even as those roles change.

### The deeper question: who am I?

This is where Vedantic inquiry begins to go further. Are we completely defined by our current role, thoughts, emotions, abilities, memories, and reputation? Or is there a deeper truth to the “I” to which all these experiences appear?

The first two layers are useful ways to examine how we live. The third is a philosophical and spiritual inquiry that cannot be settled by picking a favourite value from a list.

## 5. A Vedantic lens: the changing and the changeless

In Advaita Vedanta, the teaching ultimately concerns *Atman*, the innermost Self, and *Brahman*, ultimate reality. The tradition asks us to examine our habitual identification with the changing body, mind, thoughts, emotions, achievements, and social identities.

For example, we might say:

- “I am successful” when a project goes well.
- “I am a failure” when it goes badly.
- “I am wise” when people value our advice.
- “I know nothing” when we encounter something unfamiliar.

The circumstances and feelings are real experiences, but Vedanta invites us to investigate whether they completely define our essential nature.

This is not a shortcut to declaring, “My foundation is Atman.” It is an invitation to inquiry. The question “What is the foundation of my life?” can begin with everyday values and gradually lead to a deeper question: **Who is the ‘I’ that I am trying to understand?**

A personal reflection can support this inquiry, but it is not a substitute for careful study, reasoning, and contemplation of Vedantic teaching.

## 6. Is helping others part of my foundation?

For many people, helping others is an important source of meaning. But even this deserves gentle examination.

Imagine that someone comes to you with a problem. You help them think it through—not by simply giving them an answer, but by asking questions that help them discover their own.

Later, they return with a similar problem. You may see the situation differently now because you have learned something new. You can acknowledge that change and explore the problem together rather than pretending to have a permanent answer.

This kind of help offers more than information. It can help another person develop their own judgement.

Ask yourself:

- Do I most enjoy having the answer, sharing what I know, or helping someone become able to find their own answer?
- If nobody praised me or remembered my help, would I still want to help?
- Can I offer what I know without needing to be the person who is always right, always needed, or always remembered?
- When someone becomes independent and no longer needs the same help, what feelings arise in me?
- Can I continue to serve while accepting that my own understanding may change?

There is no need to judge your answers. Wanting to be useful, appreciated, or remembered is human. Reflection simply helps us see these motives more clearly.

## 7. Wisdom, knowledge, and recognition

It is also worth distinguishing three wishes that can look similar:

- **To become wiser:** to understand life more deeply and make more considered choices.
- **To use knowledge in service:** to share what we have learned in ways that help others.
- **To be remembered as wise:** to leave a reputation or legacy.

These wishes can coexist. None needs to be condemned. But they are different, and it can be useful to ask which matters most to you.

Imagine that you helped many people, but nobody remembered your name. Would you feel content because they benefited? Would you still value the understanding you gained? Would you feel some sadness because you hoped to leave a legacy? Or would your response be a mixture?

Try not to select the answer that sounds most noble. The honest answer is the useful starting point.

## 8. A quiet reflection practice for Day 1

Set aside five to ten quiet minutes. You may wish to look at an image of Shailaputri, sit quietly, pray, or simply contemplate the mountain image. Follow your own religious or family practice if you have one.

Write a few words for each prompt. “I don't know yet” is a valid answer.

### Questions for reflection

1. **What is the foundation of my life today?** What do I currently rely on most when making decisions or facing difficulty?
2. **What would I want to remain unchanged if my circumstances changed?** Consider qualities, commitments, relationships, or ways of responding—not only possessions or roles.
3. **What gives that something its importance?** Does it matter because it brings joy, helps others, gives me identity, earns recognition, or something else?
4. **What do I want to offer others?** Knowledge, practical help, listening, encouragement, example, or the ability to find their own answers?
5. **How do I feel when my help is not needed anymore?** What might that feeling tell me about service, usefulness, and identity?
6. **Do I want to become wise, to be useful through knowledge, or to be remembered as knowledgeable?** Are these different wishes for me?
7. **What remains when a role or achievement changes?** What do I notice about myself in that moment?
8. **What am I still searching for?** Can I allow the question to remain open without rushing to manufacture an answer?

You do not have to answer all eight questions in one sitting. Choose one that feels alive for you and return to the others later.

## 9. A simple way to write your reflection

Use this short structure if you find it helpful:

- **What I currently believe:** …
- **An everyday example that makes me think this:** …
- **What I am unsure about:** …
- **A question I want to keep exploring:** …

For example, someone might write: “I think learning is important because it lets me help others. I am unsure whether I value learning for its own sake or because it makes me feel useful. I want to explore whether I can still feel worthwhile when I do not have an answer.”

That is not a final conclusion. It is a sincere beginning.

## 10. The invitation of Day 1

Shailaputri's name brings the mountain into our contemplation. The image can remind us to ask what supports us when the weather of life changes. Religious worship of the Goddess remains meaningful in its own right; this personal reflection is one additional way to engage with the first day.

From a practical perspective, we can examine the values and qualities on which we rely. From a Vedantic perspective, we can go further and investigate the changing identities with which we usually define ourselves.

**You do not have to discover the foundation of your life in a single day.** The first step is to look honestly at what you rely on, what you hope to preserve, and why it matters to you.

A useful beginning is not a perfect answer. It is a question you are willing to explore sincerely.
