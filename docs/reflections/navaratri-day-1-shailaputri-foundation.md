# Day 1 of Navaratri — Shailaputri: What Is the Foundation of My Life?

*Part of the [Navaratri series](navaratri.md).*

> **A guided reflection for the first day of Navaratri**  
> Explore how Shailaputri, the Daughter of the Mountain, is worshipped, the traditional story behind her, the meaning of her symbols, and a Vedantic reading of "foundation". The questions are invitations, not tests. You do not need to reach a final answer today.

## 1. How the day is observed

Shailaputri is worshipped as the first of the nine forms of Goddess Durga, the Navadurga, on the first day of Navaratri. Her name joins *shaila* (mountain) and *putri* (daughter). She is the **Daughter of the Mountain**, identified with Parvati, daughter of Himavan (Himavat), the king of the Himalayas.

Customs differ widely, so what follows is a general picture, not a rule:

- **Ghatasthapana.** In many homes, the festival begins by installing a sacred pot (*kalasha*), commonly with water, a coconut and mango leaves, together with a dish in which grains are sown. It is often understood as inviting the Goddess's presence for the nine days. Details vary by region and family.
- **Temple visits and home worship.** Many families pray at home, or visit a temple of the Goddess if it is feasible.
- **Fasting.** Fasting during Navaratri is common, though its form varies.
- **A common mantra:**

  > ॐ देवी शैलपुत्र्यै नमः  
  > *Om Devi Shailaputryai Namah*  
  > "Om, salutations to the Goddess Shailaputri."

- **Regional flavours of Navaratri** (festival-wide, not specific to Day 1):
    - In **Gujarat**, evenings are filled with Garba and Dandiya.
    - In **Tamil Nadu**, many homes arrange *Golu* (Kolu), a display of dolls on steps.
    - In **Telangana and Andhra Pradesh**, women celebrate *Bathukamma* with flower arrangements, and some homes arrange *Bommala Koluvu*.
    - In **Karnataka**, Mysuru's Dasara and the *Gombe* doll displays are well known.
    - In **Maharashtra** and **Uttar Pradesh**, *Ghatasthapana* is widely observed, and some keep a lamp (*akhand jyot*) burning through the nine days.
    - In **Bengal**, the main Durga Puja days are *Shashthi* to *Dashami*, so the festival is not organised as a nine-day Navadurga sequence in the same way.
    - In **Nepal**, the festival is celebrated as *Dashain*.

I do not have a fixed family ritual for this day beyond going to the temple when possible. If your family follows a particular practice, that practice comes first. This article is an additional way to reflect, not a replacement.

## 2. The story: from Sati to Parvati

Several traditions identify Shailaputri with Parvati, born as the daughter of Himavan. In an earlier birth, the tradition says, she was **Sati**, daughter of Daksha and wife of Shiva. Daksha held a great sacrifice (*yajna*) and did not invite Shiva. Sati attended anyway, and when Daksha insulted Shiva, she could not bear it and gave up that life in the sacrificial fire.

In her next birth she was born as Parvati, the daughter of the mountain, and in time she was united with Shiva again. Parvati's own story continues on Day 2, when we meet her as Brahmacharini.

Details vary by Purana and by region, and I have kept this telling short. Some traditions also describe Shailaputri as embodying the combined power of Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva.

## 3. The form and its symbols

Traditional images show Shailaputri with two hands, a crescent moon on her forehead, a trident in the right hand and a lotus in the left, seated on the bull Nandi. **The tradition gives the form; the interpretations below are suggestions** and may differ between lineages.

| Feature | Traditional description | A possible meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Mountain parentage** | Daughter of Himavan, the Himalayas. | Steadiness and a firm beginning; the ground on which later steps depend. |
| **Trident (trishula)** in the right hand | A three-pronged weapon, also associated with Shiva. | Often read as power over the three *gunas*, or the three phases of time. Readings vary. |
| **Lotus** in the left hand | A flower that rises from mud and stays unstained by water. | Purity and beauty that grow amid ordinary conditions. |
| **Crescent moon** on the forehead | An ornament linked with Shiva. | Calm and coolness of mind; a light that waxes and wanes yet remains. |
| **Bull (Nandi) as mount** | The bull is Shiva's companion. | Steadfastness, strength and dharma, often described as the bull's symbolic role. |

In some yogic readings, she is also associated with the root centre, *Muladhara*, the base of the spiritual journey. This is a tradition-specific idea, and I mention it only as one reading.

## 4. Everyday example: when the weather changes

A mountain gives us a simple image. Weather changes around it. Clouds come and go. There may be sunshine one day and a storm the next. The mountain remains grounded.

![The Himalayas, a symbol of steadiness and a firm beginning](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1464822759023-fed622ff2c3b?auto=format&fit=crop&w=1400&q=85)

*The mountain is our reflection image for Day 1: it stands through changing weather, while the landscape around it continually changes.*

This is a **reflection inspired by the image of the mountain**, not a claim that it is the only meaning of Shailaputri.

Imagine two people who experience a setback at work. One thinks, “I have lost this opportunity, so perhaps I am no longer capable or valuable.” The setback becomes a judgement about their entire self.

The other is disappointed too, but thinks, “This matters, and I need to learn from it. My role may change, but I can still act with integrity, learn, ask for help, and begin again.”

Neither person is free from pain. The difference is what each leans on when circumstances change.

Ask yourself:

- If my job title changed, what would still matter to me?
- If I made a mistake, what would help me recover without defining myself by that mistake?
- What qualities would I hope to preserve through success and disappointment alike?

Do not choose answers because they sound spiritual. Notice what is actually true for you today.

## 5. A Vedantic lens: the changing and the changeless

The first day of a journey is a natural time to ask: *What am I building my life upon?* Think of a house. We may admire its rooms and furniture, but the foundation supports the structure. Our job, income, achievements and social roles are the visible parts of life. Beneath them are our values, habits, relationships and understanding.

It can help to distinguish three layers:

- **What I do:** work, caregiving, creating, and the roles we play. These change through life.
- **The qualities I cultivate:** honesty, courage, patience, curiosity. They shape how we perform our roles, even as the roles change.
- **The deeper question, who am I?** Are we completely defined by our role, thoughts, emotions, abilities and reputation? Or is there a deeper truth to the “I” to which all these experiences appear?

In Advaita Vedanta, the teaching concerns *Atman*, the innermost Self, and *Brahman*, ultimate reality. The tradition asks us to examine our habitual identification with the changing body, mind, thoughts, emotions and achievements. For example, we say “I am successful” when a project goes well and “I am a failure” when it goes badly. These experiences are real, but Vedanta invites us to investigate whether they completely define our essential nature.

This is not a shortcut to declaring, “My foundation is Atman.” It is an invitation to inquiry that can begin with everyday values and gradually lead to a deeper question: **Who is the ‘I’ that I am trying to understand?**

This is a **reflective reading**, not a claim that every tradition interprets the Goddess in this way. A personal reflection can support this inquiry, but it is not a substitute for careful study with a qualified teacher.

## 6. Questions for reflection

Set aside five to ten quiet minutes. You may look at an image of Shailaputri, offer a prayer, or simply contemplate the mountain image. Write a few words for each prompt. “I don't know yet” is a valid answer.

1. **What is the foundation of my life today?** What do I currently rely on most when making decisions or facing difficulty?
2. **What would I want to remain unchanged if my circumstances changed?** Consider qualities, commitments, relationships, or ways of responding—not only possessions or roles.
3. **What gives that something its importance?** Does it matter because it brings joy, helps others, gives me identity, earns recognition, or something else?
4. **What remains when a role or achievement changes?** What do I notice about myself in that moment?
5. **What am I still searching for?** Can I allow the question to remain open without rushing to manufacture an answer?

You do not have to answer all five in one sitting. Choose one that feels alive and return to the others later.

## 7. The invitation of Day 1

Shailaputri's name brings the mountain into our contemplation. The image can remind us to ask what supports us when the weather of life changes. Religious worship of the Goddess remains meaningful in its own right; this reflection is one additional way to engage with the first day.

**You do not have to discover the foundation of your life in a single day.** The first step is to look honestly at what you rely on, what you hope to preserve, and why it matters to you. A useful beginning is not a perfect answer. It is a question you are willing to explore sincerely.
