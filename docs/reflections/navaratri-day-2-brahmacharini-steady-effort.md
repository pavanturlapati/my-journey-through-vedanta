# Day 2 of Navaratri — Brahmacharini: What Does Steady Effort Look Like?

*Part of the [Navaratri series](navaratri.md).*

> **A guided reflection for the second day of Navaratri**  
> Explore how Brahmacharini is worshipped, the traditional story of Parvati's penance, the meaning of her symbols, and a Vedantic reading of *tapas* as the patient training of the mind. The questions are invitations, not tests.

## 1. How the day is observed

Brahmacharini is worshipped as the second of the nine forms of Goddess Durga, the Navadurga, on the second day (*dwitiya*) of Navaratri. Her name joins *brahma* (sacred knowledge, austerity) and *charini* (she who pursues or practises), and is commonly understood as "one who follows the path of sacred knowledge and discipline."

Customs differ widely, so what follows is a general picture, not a rule:

- **Temple visits and home worship.** Many families offer prayers at home, or visit a temple of the Goddess if it is feasible.
- **Fasting.** Fasting during Navaratri is common, though its form varies by family and region.
- **A common mantra.** Devotees often use a short salutation to the day's form of the Goddess:

  > ॐ देवी ब्रह्मचारिण्यै नमः  
  > *Om Devi Brahmacharinyai Namah*  
  > "Om, salutations to the Goddess Brahmacharini."

- **Regional flavours of Navaratri** (these are festival-wide, not specific to Day 2):
    - In **Gujarat**, evenings are filled with Garba and Dandiya.
    - In **Tamil Nadu**, many homes arrange *Golu* (Kolu), a display of dolls on steps.
    - In **Telangana and Andhra Pradesh**, women celebrate *Bathukamma* with flower arrangements, and some homes arrange *Bommala Koluvu*.
    - In **Karnataka**, Mysuru's Dasara and the *Gombe* doll displays are well known.
    - In **Maharashtra** and **Uttar Pradesh**, many homes begin with *Ghatasthapana*, installing a sacred pot, and some keep a lamp (*akhand jyot*) burning through the nine days.
    - In **Bengal**, the main Durga Puja days are *Shashthi* to *Dashami*, so the festival is not organised as a nine-day Navadurga sequence in the same way.
    - In **Nepal**, the festival is celebrated as *Dashain*.

I do not have a fixed family ritual for this day beyond going to the temple when possible. If your family follows a particular practice, that practice comes first. This article is an additional way to reflect, not a replacement.

## 2. The story: Parvati's penance

The story below is told in several Puranic and literary sources, including Kalidasa's *Kumarasambhava*, and details vary from version to version. This is one common telling.

Parvati, the daughter of Himavan, whom we met as Shailaputri yesterday, resolved that she would marry Shiva. Shiva was a solitary ascetic, absorbed in meditation, and the match did not seem likely. According to the tradition, the sage Narada advised her that if she wanted to win Shiva, she should undertake *tapas*, austerity.

So she went to the forest and began her penance. In popular tellings it lasted thousands of years (5,000 years is a figure often quoted). She lived at first on fruit and flowers, later on leaves, and eventually on very little. One name for her, **Aparna**, is explained as "one who took no sustenance, not even leaves." Another name, **Uma**, is traditionally explained from her mother's words *u mā*, "O, do not," pleading with her not to practise such severe penance.

Then, in many versions, Shiva came to test her. Disguised as a young *brahmachari*, he spoke at length against Shiva himself, listing his flaws. Parvati was not shaken. She defended him calmly and firmly. Satisfied, Shiva revealed his true form and accepted her.

Some versions add further episodes, such as Kamadeva's attempt to stir Shiva's attention, or other tests along the way. I have left those out. The core of the story is the same across tellings: **a clear aim, a long period of steady effort, and a mind that did not waver.**

## 3. The form and its symbols

Traditional images of Brahmacharini are simple, and that simplicity is part of the message. The following are the commonly described features. As always, **the tradition gives the form; the interpretations are suggestions** and may differ between lineages.

| Feature | Traditional description | A possible meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **White clothing** | She is usually shown in white. | Simplicity and purity; nothing decorative to distract from the aim. |
| **Japa mala (rosary)** in the right hand | A string of beads used to count repetitions of a mantra. | Steady repetition. Practice is made of small acts repeated patiently. |
| **Kamandalu (water pot)** in the left hand | The simple vessel of an ascetic or student. | Contentment with little; the minimal needs of a seeker. |
| **Bare feet** | She walks without footwear. | Humility and direct contact with the ground; the unadorned effort of the journey. |
| **No vahana (mount)** | Unlike Shailaputri with her bull, she is usually shown without a vehicle. | She relies on her own inner strength, not on outside support. |

Notice the contrast with Day 1. Shailaputri is the foundation. Brahmacharini is what we **do** on that foundation, day after day.

## 4. Everyday example: learning a skill slowly

Think of someone learning a musical instrument or a new language. In the first week there is excitement. By the third week the excitement fades. Progress is slow and it is easy to feel that nothing is happening.

Two people may both feel this. One stops, concluding, "I am not made for this." The other keeps a small daily practice, perhaps fifteen minutes, even on dull days. Months later, something that once felt impossible has become natural.

The second person is not more talented. They have learned to **keep going without needing constant encouragement.** That is a small picture of what the tradition calls *tapas*.

Ask yourself:

- What is one thing I have kept doing for a long time, even when it was not exciting?
- What helped me continue: a goal, a habit, a person, a belief?
- What did I stop, and what did I learn from stopping?

## 5. A Vedantic lens: tapas as the training of the mind

The word *tapas* originally suggests heat or discipline. In popular imagination it can look like extreme physical hardship. The Bhagavad Gita, in Chapter 17, gives a broader and quieter description. It speaks of austerity of the **body**, of **speech** and of the **mind**.

- **Speech (17.15):** "Words that do not cause distress, are truthful, inoffensive, and beneficial, as well as regular recitation of the Vedic scriptures—these are declared as austerity of speech." (Translation: Swami Mukundananda)
- **Mind (17.16):** "Serenity of thought, gentleness, silence, self-control, and purity of purpose." (Same translation)

> मनः प्रसादः सौम्यत्वं मौनमात्मविनिग्रहः ।  
> भावसंशुद्धिरित्येतत्तपो मानसमुच्यते ॥ 17.16

Read this way, Brahmacharini's penance is less about suffering and more about **steady, purposeful training**: a mind that is calm, gentle and clear about what it is working towards.

Two threads follow from this:

1. **Tapas trains the mind.** Vedanta asks us to examine the mind, and a restless or scattered mind is a poor instrument for inquiry. Discipline is not the goal; it prepares the instrument. For more on this, see [Vedanta Must Be Practiced](../chapter-01/07-practice-vedanta.md).
2. **Persistence without being completed by the result.** Parvati's effort continued without any guarantee. Our own goals can be pursued fully while we still ask whether they must make us complete. This is the question raised in [Desire, Self and Worldly Dependence](../chapter-01/10-desire-self-and-worldly-dependence.md).

This is a **reflective reading**, not a claim that every tradition interprets the Goddess in this way. Study with a qualified teacher remains the way to understand Vedantic teaching properly.

## 6. Questions for reflection

Set aside five to ten quiet minutes. You may look at an image of Brahmacharini, offer a prayer, or simply sit. Write a few words for each prompt. "I don't know yet" is a valid answer.

1. **What am I committed to over the long term?** What aim or practice would I be glad to still be working on a year from now?
2. **What does my steady effort look like in daily life?** What small action do I repeat, and does it happen only when I feel motivated?
3. **What tests my patience?** When do I feel like giving up, and what usually happens next?
4. **How is my mind during effort: calm or agitated?** Which of serenity, gentleness, silence or self-control do I most need to develop?
5. **What do I hope this effort will give me?** If I achieved it, would I feel complete, or would I still be asking a deeper question?

You do not have to answer all five in one sitting. Choose one that feels alive and return to the others later.

## 7. The invitation of Day 2

Brahmacharini's story is about patience more than power. The image shows a figure with almost nothing in her hands, only a rosary and a water pot, walking steadily towards what she has chosen.

Religious worship of the Goddess remains meaningful in its own right. This reflection is one additional way to engage with the day: to look honestly at what we are committed to, how steadily we work for it, and the state of the mind that does the work.

**You do not have to become an ascetic.** A small practice, repeated patiently, is a sincere place to begin.
