## 1. How the day is observed

Chandraghanta is worshipped as the third of the nine forms of Goddess Durga, the Navadurga, on the third day (*tritiya*) of Navaratri. Her name is usually explained as "adorned with the moon as her bell": *chandra* (moon) and *ghanta* (bell). She is venerated as a protector of her devotees and a destroyer of demons.

Customs differ widely, so what follows is a general picture, not a rule:

- **Temple visits and home worship.** Many families pray at home, or visit a temple of the Goddess if it is feasible.
- **Fasting.** Fasting during Navaratri is common, though its form varies by family and region.
- **A common mantra:**

  > ॐ देवी चंद्रघण्टायै नमः  
  > *Om Devi Chandraghantayai Namah*  
  > "Om, salutations to the Goddess Chandraghanta."

- **Regional flavours of Navaratri** (festival-wide, not specific to Day 3):
    - In **Punjab and Haryana**, many women make an earthen image of *Sanjhi Mata* and sing to her through the nine days, and *Kanjak*, the honouring of young girls, is observed on Ashtami.
    - In **Maharashtra**, *Bhondla* songs and dances are performed by women and girls around an image of an elephant, and the *Gondhal* musical tradition is common during Navaratri.
    - In **Bihar and Jharkhand**, some women perform the *Jhijhiya* dance with a lamp inside a pot that has many holes.
    - In **Kashmir**, many Hindu households begin by sowing barley in a pot (*Krech Puja*) and water it through the nine days.
    - In **Goa**, some temples swing the Goddess's image on a decorated silver swing, *Makharotsav*.
    - In **Kerala**, books and tools are worshipped for Saraswati in the last days, and children begin learning on Vijayadashami.

I do not have a fixed family ritual for this day beyond going to the temple when possible. If your family follows a particular practice, that practice comes first. This article is an additional way to reflect, not a replacement.

## 2. The stories: a wedding and a battle

Two stories are commonly told about Chandraghanta. They come from popular retellings, and the sources I found for them are limited, so please read them as traditional tellings that vary, not as a single fixed text.

**The wedding.** After her long penance (the story we followed on Day 2), Parvati was to marry Shiva. Shiva arrived with his wedding procession, and it was unlike any the family had seen: ash on his body, snakes around his neck, a garland of skulls, and a strange company of ghosts, spirits, ascetics and *ganas* dancing and howling behind him. Parvati's family was terrified, and the joyful occasion turned to fear and confusion.

Parvati smiled calmly. To steady her family and to bring balance to the scene, she took the form of Chandraghanta: radiant, riding a lion, with ten arms holding weapons and a crescent moon shaped like a bell on her forehead. When Shiva saw this, he asked her to return to her usual form, and she in turn asked him to take his gentle one. The wedding then went ahead.

**The battle.** In another telling, a proud asura named **Jatukasura** gained great power through penance and sent armies of giant bats that darkened the sky and disturbed prayer and rituals. The gods prayed to the Mother, and she appeared as Chandraghanta. The sound of her bell and the roar of her lion scattered the bats, and she ended the asura with her trident.

In both stories, the central image is the same: **a figure who is fully ready for danger and yet remains composed.** The bell, in particular, is a sound said to stun the wicked and reassure the devout.

## 3. The form and its symbols

In the traditional description, Chandraghanta is golden in complexion, has ten arms, and rides a lion. **The tradition gives the form; the interpretations below are suggestions** and may differ between lineages.

| Feature | Traditional description | A possible meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Crescent moon like a bell** on the forehead | The half-moon shaped like a bell gives her name. | Coolness and calm of mind, carried even in the middle of action. |
| **The bell (ghanta)** | Its sound is said to unsettle demons and comfort devotees. | A sound that cuts through noise, a call to attention and awareness. |
| **Ten arms with weapons** | Commonly described as holding a trident, mace, sword, bow and arrow, along with a lotus and a water pot. | Readiness: being prepared and capable, not helpless. |
| **Lotus and water pot** | Gentler objects held alongside the weapons. | Beside strength, a quality of grace and care. |
| **One hand in blessing (abhaya mudra)** | A raised palm, the gesture of "fear not." | Reassurance and the offer of fearlessness. |
| **Lion as mount** | Often described as symbolising bravery. | Courage that is natural rather than forced. |
| **Third eye** | An extra eye in the middle of the forehead. | Insight; seeing beyond what appears on the surface. |

Some yogic traditions associate her with the *Manipura* (navel) centre. This is one tradition-specific reading and I mention it only for completeness.

Notice what is unusual here. She is warrior-ready, yet the face is serene. Compare her with Day 2: Brahmacharini works quietly at a long aim. Chandraghanta shows the composure needed when things around us become unsettled.

## 4. Everyday example: the delayed flight

Imagine a crowded airport. A flight is cancelled and the queue is long. Voices rise, people argue, and everyone feels anxious.

One traveller joins the noise, snapping at the staff and spiralling into worry about everything that could go wrong. Another is just as inconvenienced, but breathes, asks what the options are, and speaks politely. They are not unaffected, and they are not passive. They are simply **ready without being reactive.**

The second traveller is not braver because they feel nothing. They keep a steady centre while the situation stays unsettled, and that makes clear thinking possible.

Ask yourself:

- When have I stayed calm in a situation where others lost their composure? What helped?
- When did fear make me act in a way I later regretted?
- Is there a situation in my life now where I could be both prepared and calm?

## 5. A Vedantic lens: fearlessness and the steady mind

The Bhagavad Gita, in Chapter 16, lists the qualities of a "divine nature." The very first one named is **fearlessness** (*abhayam*):

> अभयं सत्त्वसंशुद्धिर्ज्ञानयोगव्यवस्थितिः ।  
> दानं दमश्च यज्ञश्च स्वाध्यायस्तप आर्जवम् ॥ 16.1

"Fearlessness, purity of mind, steadfastness in spiritual knowledge, charity, control of the senses, sacrifice, study of the sacred books, austerity, and straightforwardness…" (Translation: Swami Mukundananda)

In his commentary on this verse, Swami Mukundananda describes fearlessness as freedom from concern for present and future miseries, and says that **inordinate attachment of any kind causes fear.** He gives examples: attachment to wealth leads to a dread of impoverishment, attachment to prestige to a fear of disgrace, and attachment to bodily comfort to a fear of ill-health.

Read this way, Chandraghanta's calm is not the absence of danger but the absence of a mind bound in fear. Two threads follow:

1. **Fear often points to what I depend on.** If I notice what I am afraid of losing, I learn something about what I am leaning on to feel complete. This is the question raised in [Desire, Self and Worldly Dependence](../chapter-01/10-desire-self-and-worldly-dependence.md): can I pursue what matters to me without asking it to complete me?
2. **A steady mind is a prepared mind.** Vedanta asks us to examine the mind, and a mind that panics cannot inquire well. Calm is not the goal in itself; it is part of preparing the instrument. See [Vedanta Must Be Practiced](../chapter-01/07-practice-vedanta.md).

This is a **reflective reading**, not a claim that every tradition interprets the Goddess in this way. Fearlessness in Vedanta ultimately rests on knowledge of the Self, which is learned through careful study with a qualified teacher, not through effort to "feel brave" alone.

## 6. Questions for reflection

Set aside five to ten quiet minutes. You may look at an image of Chandraghanta, offer a prayer, or simply sit. Write a few words for each prompt. "I don't know yet" is a valid answer.

1. **What am I afraid of right now?** Name it plainly, whether large or small.
2. **How do I behave when things become unsettled?** What do I tend to do first: freeze, react, withdraw, or take charge?
3. **What helps me stay steady?** Which people, habits, breaths or words bring me back to a calm centre?
4. **What is my fear showing me that I depend on?** If I look beneath the fear, what am I afraid of losing?
5. **What would calm courage look like for me this week?** What is one small, concrete way to be ready without being reactive?

You do not have to answer all five in one sitting. Choose one that feels alive and return to the others later.

## 7. The invitation of Day 3

Chandraghanta's image holds two things together that we often think are opposites: readiness and calm. The ten arms are prepared, and the moon on her forehead is cool.

Religious worship of the Goddess remains meaningful in its own right. This reflection is one additional way to engage with the day: to notice what we fear, how we respond when things are unsettled, and what a steady centre could look like.

**You do not have to become fearless today.** Noticing one fear honestly, and one thing that helps you stay steady, is a sincere place to begin.
