# Day 9 of Navaratri — Siddhidatri: What Does Fulfilment Really Mean?

*Part of the [Navaratri series](navaratri.md).*

> **A guided reflection for the ninth day of Navaratri**  
> Explore how Siddhidatri is worshipped, the traditional account of the Goddess as giver of *siddhis*, the meaning of her symbols, and a Vedantic reading of fulfilment as knowing the Self. The questions are invitations, not tests.

## 1. How the day is observed

Siddhidatri (*Siddhidātrī*) is worshipped as the ninth and last of the nine forms of Goddess Durga, the Navadurga, on the ninth day of Navaratri, commonly called *Navami* or *Mahanavami*. Her name means "the giver of *siddhis*": *siddhi* is commonly translated as accomplishment, perfection or supernatural power, and *dātrī* means the one who gives.

Customs differ widely, so what follows is a general picture, not a rule:

- **Temple visits and home worship.** Many families pray at home, or visit a temple of the Goddess if it is feasible.
- **Fasting and its conclusion.** Fasting during Navaratri is common, and in many families the fast is concluded around this day or the next. Practice varies.
- **Kanya Puja.** In many parts of North India, young girls are honoured as forms of the Goddess on Ashtami or Navami, as described on Day 8.
- **Ayudha Puja.** In parts of South India, tools, books, vehicles and instruments of one's work are worshipped on Navami. Many people place their books and tools aside for the day as a gesture of gratitude.
- **A common mantra:**

  > ॐ देवी सिद्धिदात्र्यै नमः  
  > *Om Devi Siddhidatryai Namah*  
  > "Om, salutations to the Goddess Siddhidatri."

- **Regional flavours of Navaratri** (festival-wide, not specific to Day 9):
    - In **Gujarat**, *Garba* and *Dandiya Raas* are danced in the evenings through the nine nights.
    - In **West Bengal**, the festival is celebrated as *Durga Puja*, with elaborate community pandals, mostly in the last days.
    - In **Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh**, many homes display *Golu* or *Bommala Koluvu*, a tiered arrangement of dolls.
    - In **Karnataka**, Mysuru's *Dasara* is known for its processions and palace celebrations.

I do not have a fixed family ritual for this day beyond going to the temple when possible. If your family follows a particular practice, that practice comes first. This article is an additional way to reflect, not a replacement.

## 2. The story: the Goddess who completes

One common telling, drawn from the *Devi Purana* tradition, says that Shiva obtained all the *siddhis* by worshipping this form of the Goddess. In this telling, through her grace, half of Shiva's body became that of the Goddess, and he came to be known as *Ardhanarishvara*, the Lord who is half woman. I could not confirm the exact source text, so please treat this as a devotional tradition.

The traditional list of *siddhis* is eight: *aṇimā*, *mahimā*, *garimā*, *laghimā*, *prāpti*, *prākāmya*, *īśitva* and *vaśitva*. Traditionally they are described as powers such as becoming very small, very large, heavy or light, reaching anywhere, fulfilling one's will, mastery and control. Some lists differ.

It is also a quiet point of this day that she appears last. After eight forms and eight days, the culmination is not a battle, but completion.

Versions and emphasis vary between texts, lineages and regions, so please read this as one common telling.

## 3. The form and its symbols

In the traditional description, Siddhidatri has four arms and is seated on a lotus. A lion is also commonly described as her mount in some accounts. **The tradition gives the form; the interpretations below are suggestions** and may differ between lineages. Published descriptions are not fully uniform, so details such as which hand holds which object vary.

| Feature | Traditional description | A possible meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Lotus seat** | She is seated on a lotus. | Being rooted and yet unstained; completion that is calm, not proud. |
| **Conch** | Commonly described in one hand. | The sacred sound, and an announcement of what is true. |
| **Discus (chakra)** | Commonly described in one hand. | The turning of time, and the power to cut through what is false. |
| **Mace** | Commonly described in one hand. | Strength that is held, not used for show. |
| **Lotus in hand** | Commonly described in one hand. | Purity and the unfolding of the heart. |
| **Lion as mount** | Described in some accounts. | Courage that has been carried through the nine days. |
| **Serene, smiling face** | A calm and gracious expression. | Contentment. |

Because the descriptions vary, I do not read much into which object is in which hand. What stands out in every version is the seat of the lotus and the calm face.

Compare her with Day 1. Shailaputri begins the journey on the foundation of the mountain. Siddhidatri completes the cycle. Each day has asked a question, and this day asks what the whole journey was for.

## 4. Everyday example: the finished project

Imagine someone who has spent months working on a long project. They hoped that finishing it would bring a deep sense of satisfaction. On the last day, they send it in, and there is a small glow of pride.

By the next morning, though, a new task is already waiting. The satisfaction was real, but shorter than expected. What lasts longer is something else: the quiet knowledge that they did the work with care, and the people who helped along the way. **The fulfilment was less in the result than in who they became while working.**

Ask yourself:

- What have I achieved that I thought would make me feel complete, and how long did that feeling last?
- When have I felt a deeper contentment, and what was present at that time?
- If I could not add anything more to my life, what would I still be grateful for?

## 5. A Vedantic lens: the real *siddhi*

In the Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 18, verse 46, Krishna says that a person attains perfection (*siddhi*) by worshipping the Divine through their own work:

> स्वे स्वे कर्मण्यभिरतः संसिद्धिं लभते नरः ।

A common reading is: "Devoted to their own duty, a person attains perfection." Chapter 18 goes on to describe the highest perfection as steadiness in knowledge of Brahman.

Read this way, Siddhidatri's image suggests two threads:

1. **Siddhi is not a show of power.** The tradition lists the eight classical *siddhis*, and also says that attachment to powers can become a distraction on the path. Vedanta places its emphasis on self-knowledge, not on extraordinary abilities. See [Vedanta Must Be Practiced](../chapter-01/07-practice-vedanta.md).
2. **Fulfilment is found by recognising what is already complete.** Vedanta describes the Self as *pūrṇa*, whole. The prayer "*pūrṇam adaḥ pūrṇam idam*" from the Isha Upanishad's peace invocation speaks of fullness in this spirit. On this reading, what the nine days have been clearing away is what hid that wholeness. See [Desire, Self and Worldly Dependence](../chapter-01/10-desire-self-and-worldly-dependence.md).

This is a **reflective reading**, not a claim that every tradition interprets the Goddess in this way. The deeper Vedantic point is understood through careful study with a qualified teacher and not through a single verse or an image.

## 6. Questions for reflection

Set aside five to ten quiet minutes. You may look at an image of Siddhidatri, offer a prayer, or simply sit. Write a few words for each prompt. "I don't know yet" is a valid answer.

1. **What does fulfilment mean to me?** If I imagine feeling truly complete, what is present in that picture?
2. **What have I already received?** Which things in my life did I once hope for and now take for granted?
3. **Which "powers" or achievements do I chase?** What am I hoping they will give me?
4. **What has changed in me over these nine days?** Even a small shift counts.
5. **What will I carry forward?** One quality, practice or question from this series that I would like to keep.

You do not have to answer all five in one sitting. Choose one that feels alive and return to the others later.

## 7. The invitation of Day 9

Siddhidatri's image is a calm face on a lotus at the end of nine days. The journey from foundation to fulfilment does not end in a show of power, but in steadiness.

Religious worship of the Goddess remains meaningful in its own right. This reflection is one additional way to engage with the day: to notice what I already have, what I have been chasing, and what I want to carry forward.

**You do not have to feel complete today.** Noticing one thing you already have, and being grateful for it, is a sincere place to end, and to begin again.
