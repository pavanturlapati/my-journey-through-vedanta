# Day 10 — Vijayadashami: What Does Victory Really Mean?

*Part of the [Navaratri series](navaratri.md).*

> **A guided reflection for the tenth day, Vijayadashami**  
> Vijayadashami follows the nine nights and is not one of the nine forms. Explore how the day is observed, the traditional stories associated with it, the meaning of its symbols, and a Vedantic reading of victory as mastery of the inner opponent. The questions are invitations, not tests.

## 1. How the day is observed

Vijayadashami (*Vijayā-daśamī*), "the tenth day of victory", is also called Dussehra or Dasara. It is observed on the tenth day, after the nine nights of Navaratri. Many traditions see it as the day on which the festival's themes are brought to a close.

Customs differ widely, so what follows is a general picture, not a rule:

- **Temple visits and home worship.** Many families pray at home, or visit a temple if it is feasible.
- **North India: Ramlila and Ravana effigies.** In many places the story of Rama is performed over the preceding days, and on this day large effigies of Ravana are burnt.
- **West Bengal and eastern India.** The Durga idols are commonly immersed in rivers or water bodies on this day, after the closing rituals of Durga Puja. Married women commonly apply vermilion on each other in a custom called *Sindoor Khela*.
- **Karnataka.** Mysuru's Dasara is known for its grand procession, the *Jamboo Savari*.
- **South India.** Many families treat the day as especially auspicious for beginning learning, and children are commonly introduced to reading and writing. In Kerala this is called *Vidyarambham*.
- **Shami tree.** In parts of western and southern India, leaves of the *shami* (or *apta*) tree are exchanged as a symbol of goodwill.
- **A common mantra:**

  > ॐ अपराजितायै नमः  
  > *Om Aparajitayai Namah*  
  > "Om, salutations to the Unconquered Goddess."

  *Aparajita*, "the unconquered", is a name of the Goddess that is traditionally worshipped on this day in some regions.

I do not have a fixed family ritual for this day beyond going to the temple when possible. If your family follows a particular practice, that practice comes first. This article is an additional way to reflect, not a replacement.

## 2. The stories: more than one victory

The day is linked with several stories, and which one is told first depends on the region. Versions vary, so please read each as one common telling.

1. **Durga and Mahishasura.** In the Devi tradition, the day marks the victory of the Goddess over the buffalo-demon Mahishasura, after the nine nights of battle (see Day 6).
2. **Rama and Ravana.** In the Ramayana tradition, the day is remembered as the day Rama defeated Ravana, the ten-headed king of Lanka, and so the effigies are shown with ten heads.
3. **The Pandavas and the shami tree.** In a tradition associated with the Mahabharata, the Pandavas are said to have hidden their weapons in a shami tree during their year of exile in disguise, and to have taken them back down on this day. This is why the tree is worshipped in some places.

What the stories share is not only that someone wins. In each, the victory comes after a long period of preparation, discipline or waiting.

## 3. The symbols of the day

The day has no single form, so here the symbols are drawn from the customs above. **The tradition gives the practices; the interpretations below are suggestions** and may differ between lineages.

| Symbol | Traditional association | A possible meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Ravana's ten heads** | Shown on effigies burnt on the day. | One common reading takes them as ten inner tendencies, such as anger, pride, greed and envy. This is a popular reading, not a fixed doctrine. |
| **Burning the effigy** | A public ritual in North India. | Letting go of what has run its course. |
| **Immersion of the idol** | A custom in Bengal and eastern India. | Form returning to the formless: the Goddess is honoured in an image, and then the image is released. |
| **Shami leaves** | Exchanged in some regions. | Goodwill, and patience that eventually bears fruit. |
| **Books, pens and tools** | Worshipped in many South Indian homes on the preceding day or this day. | Gratitude for the instruments of one's work and learning. |
| **The first letters** | Children's first writing in some regions. | A beginning made with care. |

Compare this day with Day 9. Siddhidatri asks what fulfilment means. Vijayadashami asks what victory means, and who has actually been defeated.

## 4. Everyday example: the argument won and lost

Imagine two people in a disagreement. One of them is quick, sharp and sure of the facts, and wins the argument cleanly. Everyone in the room can see who was right.

That night, though, the winner feels uneasy. Something was said more harshly than it needed to be, and a friendship has cooled. The next day, the other person quietly says, "I think you were right." Neither feels better. **Some victories leave us smaller, and some quiet defeats are what a mind needs to grow.**

Ask yourself:

- When have I "won" something and felt strangely empty afterwards?
- When did I lose something, and later see that it was a good thing?
- Who, or what, did I think I was fighting, and who was I really fighting?

## 5. A Vedantic lens: the victory that is inside

The last verse of the Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 18, verse 78, speaks of victory:

> यत्र योगेश्वरः कृष्णो यत्र पार्थो धनुर्धरः ।  
> तत्र श्रीर्विजयो भूतिर्ध्रुवा नीतिर्मतिर्मम ॥ 18.78

A common reading is: "Wherever there is Krishna, the Lord of yoga, and Arjuna, the archer, there will be prosperity, victory, well-being and firm righteousness. This is my conviction." (Words of Sanjaya.) A reflective reading is that victory here is tied to the union of right guidance and right action, not to strength alone.

Read this way, Vijayadashami suggests two threads:

1. **The decisive victory is over the inner opponent.** Across the series, the opponent has appeared as ego, fear, anger, desire and clinging. In the Gita, as in Day 6, desire and anger are named as the great enemy. If the ten heads of Ravana can be read as ten tendencies, then the burning of the effigy points at something one does not leave to the crowd. See [Desire, Self and Worldly Dependence](../chapter-01/10-desire-self-and-worldly-dependence.md).
2. **The victory is not for a "me" to own.** In Vedanta, the sense of being the doer is itself examined. The Gita's verse places victory where right knowledge and right action meet, and a victory held lightly does not need an enemy to keep it alive. See [Vedanta Must Be Practiced](../chapter-01/07-practice-vedanta.md).

This is a **reflective reading**, not a claim that every tradition interprets the festival in this way. The deeper Vedantic point is understood through careful study with a qualified teacher and not through a single verse or an image.

## 6. Questions for reflection

Set aside five to ten quiet minutes. You may look at an image of the Goddess, offer a prayer, or simply sit. Write a few words for each prompt. "I don't know yet" is a valid answer.

1. **What does victory mean to me?** When I picture a real win, what is in that picture?
2. **Which of my "ten heads" is the strongest right now?** Is it anger, pride, envy, greed, laziness or something else?
3. **What is something I "won" that left me feeling smaller?** What would I do differently?
4. **What did the last nine days show me?** Which day's question stayed with me most, and why?
5. **What is the one thing I want to begin, or keep going, now that the nights are over?** What would a first step look like?

You do not have to answer all five in one sitting. Choose one that feels alive and return to the others later.

## 7. The invitation of Day 10

Vijayadashami closes the nine nights with a bright image: a victory after patient preparation. The festival that started with a foundation ends with a question about what has really been won.

Religious worship of the Goddess remains meaningful in its own right. This reflection is one additional way to engage with the day: to notice the inner opponent, to hold a win lightly, and to begin one small thing well.

**You do not have to defeat everything in you today.** Naming the opponent that matters most, and deciding on one small step toward mastering it, is a sincere place to begin.
