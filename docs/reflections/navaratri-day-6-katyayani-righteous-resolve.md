# Day 6 of Navaratri — Katyayani: What Am I Called to Stand Up For?

*Part of the [Navaratri series](navaratri.md).*

> **A guided reflection for the sixth day of Navaratri**  
> Explore how Katyayani is worshipped, the traditional account of her emergence to defeat Mahishasura, the meaning of her symbols, and a Vedantic reading of anger and desire as the inner opponents. The questions are invitations, not tests.

## 1. How the day is observed

Katyayani (*Kātyāyanī*) is worshipped as the sixth of the nine forms of Goddess Durga, the Navadurga, on the sixth day of Navaratri. Her name is commonly connected with the sage Katyayana, who is said to have worshipped the Goddess and asked that she be born as his daughter. She is often described as a warrior form of the Goddess.

Customs differ widely, so what follows is a general picture, not a rule:

- **Temple visits and home worship.** Many families pray at home, or visit a temple of the Goddess if it is feasible.
- **Fasting.** Fasting during Navaratri is common, though its form varies by family and region.
- **Marriage prayers.** In some traditions, Katyayani is prayed to for a suitable marriage. A well-known reference is the account of the *gopis* of Vrindavan who, in the Bhagavata Purana, are described as worshipping her.
- **A common mantra:**

  > ॐ देवी कात्यायन्यै नमः  
  > *Om Devi Katyayanyai Namah*  
  > "Om, salutations to the Goddess Katyayani."

- **Regional flavours of Navaratri** (festival-wide, not specific to Day 6):
    - In **Gujarat**, *Garba* and *Dandiya Raas* are danced in the evenings through the nine nights.
    - In **West Bengal**, the festival is celebrated as *Durga Puja*, with elaborate community pandals, mostly in the last days.
    - In **Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh**, many homes display *Golu* or *Bommala Koluvu*, a tiered arrangement of dolls.
    - In **Karnataka**, Mysuru's *Dasara* is known for its processions and palace celebrations.

I do not have a fixed family ritual for this day beyond going to the temple when possible. If your family follows a particular practice, that practice comes first. This article is an additional way to reflect, not a replacement.

## 2. The story: when the gods' anger became a form

One common telling, found in the Puranic tradition, says that the buffalo-demon Mahishasura had received a boon that made him very hard to defeat, and he troubled the gods. In their distress, the gods are said to have focused their energy, and this combined radiance took the form of the Goddess. The sage Katyayana worshipped her first, and for this reason she is known as Katyayani.

In this telling she then defeated Mahishasura, and she is therefore also called *Mahishasuramardini*, "the one who crushes Mahishasura." The longer account of the battle is told in the *Devi Mahatmya* (Durga Saptashati).

It is worth noticing that she does not come from one person's strength. She arises when many people's energy is brought into one purpose.

Versions and emphasis vary between texts, lineages and regions, so please read this as one common telling.

## 3. The form and its symbols

In the traditional description, Katyayani has four arms (some descriptions give more) and rides a lion. **The tradition gives the form; the interpretations below are suggestions** and may differ between lineages. Published descriptions are not fully uniform, so details such as which hand holds which object vary.

| Feature | Traditional description | A possible meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Lion as mount** | A lion is commonly described as her vehicle. | Courage and strength put to use for a purpose. |
| **Sword** | Commonly described in one of her left hands. | Discernment that cuts through confusion. |
| **Lotus** | Commonly described in another left hand. | Purity and calm, even in the middle of the struggle. |
| **Raised hand (abhaya mudra)** | One right hand is shown in a gesture of protection. | Reassurance: "do not be afraid." |
| **Boon-giving hand (varada mudra)** | Another right hand grants blessings. | Strength is meant to serve, not to dominate. |
| **Golden, radiant complexion** | Often described as luminous. | Clarity of purpose. |

The sword and the lotus together are worth pausing over: one for decisive action, the other for gentleness.

Compare her with Day 5. Skandamata shows care that holds and protects a child. Katyayani shows the strength that stands up when something must be stopped.

## 4. Everyday example: the quiet "no"

Imagine a person who has always gone along with a group decision, even when they disagreed. One day, they see that a colleague is being treated unfairly in a meeting. Their heart beats fast. They could stay silent, as they always have, or they could speak.

They speak, politely and plainly. There is no shouting, only a clear sentence: "I don't think that's fair, and I'd like us to look at it again."

The meeting goes quiet, then moves on. Nothing dramatic happens, but something has shifted. **Strength does not have to be loud to be real. It has to be clear.**

Ask yourself:

- When did I last stay silent about something I believed was wrong?
- What is a value I would not trade away, even if it cost me some comfort?
- Is there something in me, a habit, a fear or an excuse, that I need to stand up to?

## 5. A Vedantic lens: the inner opponent

In the Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 3, Arjuna asks what drives a person to act wrongly, almost as if forced. Krishna's answer, in verse 37, names **desire and anger** (*kāma* and *krodha*) as the great enemy. The chapter closes in verse 43 with a call to master the self by the Self and to defeat this enemy:

> एवं बुद्धेः परं बुद्ध्वा संस्तभ्यात्मानमात्मना ।  
> जहि शत्रुं महाबाहो कामरूपं दुरासदम् ॥ 3.43

Read this way, Mahishasura can be seen as a picture of an inner force, not only an outer one. Two threads follow:

1. **The first battle is inside.** Before I can stand up for something in the world, I need to see what pulls me off course: anger, craving, fear of disapproval. Katyayani's sword can be read as discernment, the ability to tell what is true from what is merely loud. See [Desire, Self and Worldly Dependence](../chapter-01/10-desire-self-and-worldly-dependence.md).
2. **Resolve and calm can sit together.** The lotus in her hand suggests that firm action need not come from rage. Vedanta asks whether I can act clearly without being swept along by the anger. See [Vedanta Must Be Practiced](../chapter-01/07-practice-vedanta.md).

This is a **reflective reading**, not a claim that every tradition interprets the Goddess in this way. The deeper Vedantic point is understood through careful study with a qualified teacher and not through a single verse or an image.

## 6. Questions for reflection

Set aside five to ten quiet minutes. You may look at an image of Katyayani, offer a prayer, or simply sit. Write a few words for each prompt. "I don't know yet" is a valid answer.

1. **What do I stand for?** Which two or three values would I find hard to give up?
2. **Where have I stayed silent when I should have spoken?** What held me back?
3. **What is my inner opponent?** Is it anger, craving, fear, laziness or something else that most often pulls me off course?
4. **Can I be firm without being harsh?** What would it look like to act clearly, with a lotus in one hand?
5. **What is one small, brave step I can take this week?** It might be a conversation, a boundary or a habit.

You do not have to answer all five in one sitting. Choose one that feels alive and return to the others later.

## 7. The invitation of Day 6

Katyayani's image holds two things together that we often keep apart: strength and calm. The sword is raised, and the lotus is still in her hand.

Religious worship of the Goddess remains meaningful in its own right. This reflection is one additional way to engage with the day: to notice what I stand for, what holds me back, and what inner opponent I most need to face.

**You do not have to fight anyone today.** Naming one value you want to live by, and taking one small step toward it, is a sincere place to begin.
