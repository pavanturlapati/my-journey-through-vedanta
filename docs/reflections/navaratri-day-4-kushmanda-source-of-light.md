# Day 4 of Navaratri — Kushmanda: Where Does My Light Come From?

*Part of the [Navaratri series](navaratri.md).*

> **A guided reflection for the fourth day of Navaratri**  
> Explore how Kushmanda is worshipped, the traditional account of the cosmic egg created by her smile, the meaning of her symbols, and a Vedantic reading of light as something we borrow and do not own. The questions are invitations, not tests.

## 1. How the day is observed

Kushmanda (*Kūṣmāṇḍā*) is worshipped as the fourth of the nine forms of Goddess Durga, the Navadurga, on the fourth day of Navaratri. The Devi Kavacha, traditionally recited with the Durga Saptashati, names her fourth in the sequence: Shailaputri, Brahmacharini, Chandraghanta, Kushmanda, and so on. Her name is commonly explained in two ways: through the *cosmic egg* (*anda*) that she is said to have brought forth, and through the word *kushmanda*, which is also used for a pumpkin or gourd.

Customs differ widely, so what follows is a general picture, not a rule:

- **Temple visits and home worship.** Many families pray at home, or visit a temple of the Goddess if it is feasible.
- **Fasting.** Fasting during Navaratri is common, though its form varies by family and region.
- **Offerings.** Some traditions associate the offering of a gourd or pumpkin with this day, in line with her name.
- **A common mantra:**

  > ॐ देवी कूष्माण्डायै नमः  
  > *Om Devi Kushmandayai Namah*  
  > "Om, salutations to the Goddess Kushmanda."

- **Varanasi.** The Durga Kund temple in Varanasi is locally identified as a centre of Kushmanda worship, especially on the fourth day. Temple guides describe the present shrine as eighteenth-century, but I could only find devotional sources for this, so please treat it as a local tradition.
- **Regional flavours of Navaratri** (festival-wide, not specific to Day 4):
    - In **Gujarat**, *Garba* and *Dandiya Raas* are danced in the evenings through the nine nights.
    - In **West Bengal**, the festival is celebrated as *Durga Puja*, with elaborate community pandals, mostly in the last days.
    - In **Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh**, many homes display *Golu* or *Bommala Koluvu*, a tiered arrangement of dolls.
    - In **Karnataka**, Mysuru's *Dasara* is known for its processions and palace celebrations.
    - In **Himachal Pradesh**, the Kullu Dussehra begins on Vijayadashami.

I do not have a fixed family ritual for this day beyond going to the temple when possible. If your family follows a particular practice, that practice comes first. This article is an additional way to reflect, not a replacement.

## 2. The story: a smile in the darkness

Unlike some of the other forms, Kushmanda has no long story of battle or penance. The traditional account, as presented in the *Nava Durga* text (Gita Press), is about **creation itself**.

Before anything existed, there was no manifested universe, and darkness extended in every direction. In that darkness, the Goddess smiled, a gentle smile, and from it the **cosmic egg** appeared. From that egg the ordered universe came to be. Light, in this telling, begins with her.

The same tradition places her in the **solar sphere**, as the power that can endure the Sun's radiance and from whom the radiance of other beings is said to derive. She is called *Sūryamaṇḍala-antarvāsinī*, "she who dwells within the solar sphere." It is worth noticing the careful wording: she is described as the power working *within* the Sun, not as another name for the solar deity Surya.

Versions and emphasis vary between texts, lineages and regions, so please read this as one common telling.

## 3. The form and its symbols

In the traditional description, Kushmanda has eight arms (hence the name *Ashtabhuja Devi*) and rides a lion. **The tradition gives the form; the interpretations below are suggestions** and may differ between lineages. Published descriptions are not fully uniform: some give more arms, mention a trident or sword, or describe a tiger rather than a lion.

| Feature | Traditional description | A possible meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **A gentle smile** | The smile from which the cosmos arises. | Creation without strain; effort that comes from fullness and not from anxiety. |
| **Eight arms** | Commonly described as holding a water pot, bow, arrow, lotus, a vessel of nectar, discus, mace and a rosary. | A wide range of capacities held together: action, protection, nourishment and devotion. |
| **Water pot (kamandalu)** | One of her objects in most descriptions. | Nourishment and sustenance; what keeps life going. |
| **Bow and arrow** | Held in two of the hands. | Focus and direction; knowing what I am aiming at. |
| **Lotus** | Held in one hand. | Something that grows in mud and yet stays unstained; beauty and grace. |
| **Vessel of nectar** | A pot said to hold *amrita*. | Vitality and well-being. |
| **Discus and mace** | Weapons carried by the form. | Protection of what has been created. |
| **Rosary (japa mala)** | Said in the account to be associated with the eight *siddhis* and nine *nidhis*. | Steady inner practice that sustains outward work. |
| **Lion as mount** | Often described as symbolising courage. | Strength that carries the work forward. |
| **The solar sphere** | The Goddess is said to dwell in the Sun. | Radiance that illuminates without being exhausted. |

Some yogic traditions associate her with the *Anahata* (heart) centre. This is one tradition-specific reading, and I mention it only for completeness.

The traditional prayer for the day describes her as holding two vessels in her lotus-like hands:

> सुरासम्पूर्णकलशं रुधिराप्लुतमेव च ।  
> दधाना हस्तपद्माभ्यां कूष्माण्डा शुभदास्तु मे ॥

Published English translations differ about the vessels, one reading the pitcher as filled with divine nectar and another as drenched in blood, so I will not choose between them here. The closing request, that Kushmanda grant auspiciousness (*shubhada*), is the clear part.

Compare her with Day 3. Chandraghanta shows composure in the midst of danger. Kushmanda points to the source from which light and life arise at all.

## 4. Everyday example: the cook who lights up the kitchen

Imagine someone preparing a meal for a large family gathering. The kitchen is busy, the plan is not exact, and there is a lot to do.

One person works with a tight jaw, counting each task as a burden and wanting thanks at the end. Another is just as busy, but hums while cooking, tastes, adjusts and smiles at whoever walks in. The room seems warmer around them. Their work is not easier. It simply **comes from a place of fullness, not of strain.**

And at the end of the meal, the cook may say, "I only did what I could with what I was given." The vegetables came from soil and sunlight, the recipe from a grandmother, the skills from years of practice. **Very little of what we create comes from us alone.**

Ask yourself:

- When have I created something, large or small, and felt that I was working from joy rather than pressure?
- Whose light, effort or kindness was behind something I am proud of?
- Is there something I could begin this week, not out of obligation, but with a gentle sense of "why not"?

## 5. A Vedantic lens: borrowed light

The Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 15, verse 12, speaks about light in a way that fits this day closely:

> यदादित्यगतं तेजो जगद्भासयतेऽखिलम् ।  
> यच्चन्द्रमसि यच्चाग्नौ तत्तेजो विद्धि मामकम् ॥ 15.12

"Know that I am like the brilliance of the sun that illuminates the entire solar system. The radiance of the moon and the brightness of the fire also come from Me." (Translation: Swami Mukundananda)

Swami Mukundananda's commentary on this verse says that we easily forget our origin, identify with the body and its relationships, and forget that the whole creation, even the Sun's radiance, is a manifestation of God's energy.

Read this way, Kushmanda's image is not far from the verse: the Sun is the brightest thing we know, and yet the tradition says its brilliance is not its own. Two threads follow:

1. **Whatever shines in my life is borrowed.** My talents, health, opportunities and even my attention arrive from sources I did not make. This does not diminish me. It changes how I hold my achievements: with gratitude, and with less of the sense that I must defend them. See [Desire, Self and Worldly Dependence](../chapter-01/10-desire-self-and-worldly-dependence.md) for the related question of what I ask my achievements to do for me.
2. **Creation comes from fullness.** In the traditional account, the Goddess creates with a smile, not from need. Vedanta asks a similar question of our own action: can I act fully, and yet not from lack? See [Vedanta Must Be Practiced](../chapter-01/07-practice-vedanta.md).

This is a **reflective reading**, not a claim that every tradition interprets the Goddess in this way. The deeper Vedantic point, that the same awareness illuminates all experience, is understood through careful study with a qualified teacher and not through a single verse or an image.

## 6. Questions for reflection

Set aside five to ten quiet minutes. You may look at an image of Kushmanda, offer a prayer, or simply sit. Write a few words for each prompt. "I don't know yet" is a valid answer.

1. **What am I creating or beginning in my life right now?** It may be small: a habit, a relationship, a project, a meal, a conversation.
2. **Does my effort come from fullness or from strain?** What changes in me when I act without anxiety about the result?
3. **Where does my light come from?** Which people, teachers, circumstances or abilities made it possible for me to do what I do well?
4. **Whom or what do I need to thank?** Is there a source of light in my life that I have never acknowledged?
5. **How could I be a gentle source of light for someone this week?** What is one small, concrete act of warmth, encouragement or help?

You do not have to answer all five in one sitting. Choose one that feels alive and return to the others later.

## 7. The invitation of Day 4

Kushmanda's image holds two things together that we often keep apart: creative energy and quiet humility. The smile brings forth a universe, and yet the radiance is described as flowing through her, not as something she hoards.

Religious worship of the Goddess remains meaningful in its own right. This reflection is one additional way to engage with the day: to notice what I am creating, whether I create from fullness or strain, and where my light comes from.

**You do not have to create something great today.** Noticing one source of light in your life, and thanking it, is a sincere place to begin.
