# Dhanteras, Diwali and Gujarati New Year — What Do We Carry Forward?

*Explore more [festival articles](../articles/index.md#festivals).*

> **A guided reflection on prosperity, light and renewal**  
> Buying gold when possible, visiting the temple, lighting diyas and gathering with family can become occasions for gratitude and understanding. The questions are invitations, not tests.

## 1. How we observe these festivals

For our family, these celebrations have familiar expressions. On **Dhanteras**, we try to see whether we can buy some gold. On **Diwali**, we visit the temple, light diyas at home, fire crackers, perform vehicle pooja and share sweets with neighbours. For **Gujarati New Year**, we wish our family a happy new year, spend time together and enjoy good food.

There is joy in these things themselves: a home lit by small flames, something sweet taken next door, a greeting that becomes a conversation, a meal enjoyed together. As I study Vedanta, I also want to ask what these familiar actions might help me notice.

### Dhanteras: welcoming prosperity

Dhanteras is commonly associated with auspicious purchases, including gold, silver and utensils. These customs express a welcome to prosperity; they are not a requirement that every household must make a purchase. [India's Ministry of Tourism describes Dhanteras purchases](https://www.incredibleindia.gov.in/en/festivals-and-events/diwali) as part of the wider Diwali celebrations.

In our home, the phrase is **“if we can.”** We see whether buying some gold is possible. I can honour that custom while also remembering the resources already supporting our lives: health, time, skills and people who care for us. A purchase may mark the occasion; gratitude can accompany it whether or not we buy anything.

### Diwali: worship, celebration and sharing

Diwali brings together lamps, prayer, celebration and hospitality, with different emphases across regions and families. Lakshmi worship is important in many households. [The Ministry of Tourism's Diwali overview](https://www.incredibleindia.gov.in/en/festivals-and-events/diwali) describes this diversity of celebration.

Our temple visit gives us time for worship. At home, lighting diyas and firing crackers are part of our celebration. Vehicle pooja is also part of how our family observes Diwali; other families may honour vehicles or tools on different occasions.

Sharing sweets with neighbours gives the celebration a wider circle. It makes room for a small encounter beyond our own household. I can bring the same attention to that meeting that I bring to preparing the sweets.

### Gujarati New Year: beginning with people

Gujarati New Year, commonly called *Bestu Varas*, begins on Kartak Sud Ekam, the first day of the bright fortnight of Kartak in the Gujarati calendar. It belongs to the festive period around Diwali; this is a particular regional new year, and Hindu calendars do not all begin together. See [this account of Bestu Varas and its customs](https://allaboutgujarat.in/culture/festivals/bestu-varas/).

For us, it means wishing family a happy new year, spending time together and eating nice food. A greeting such as **નૂતન વર્ષાભિનંદન — Nutan Varshabhinandan**, “New Year greetings,” gives words to that wish. The time we give one another can give it substance.

These are our family's practices. Your own family or lineage may observe these occasions differently; this reflection is an additional invitation to contemplate them.

## 2. The stories behind the celebrations

### Dhanvantari and the churning of the ocean

One traditional account associated with Dhanteras recalls **Dhanvantari**, revered in Ayurveda as the divine physician. In the story of *samudra manthana*, the churning of the ocean of milk, he emerges carrying a vessel of *amrita*, the nectar of immortality. The [Arya Vaidya Chikitsalayam's account of its Dhanvantari temple](https://www.avcri.org/dhanwantharitemple) describes this tradition. Versions of the wider ocean-churning narrative vary.

Alongside the custom of buying gold, this story gives me a reason to pause over health and care. What allows me to enjoy what I own? Who helps sustain my life? These are personal reflections prompted by the story, rather than an explanation of every Dhanteras custom.

### Rama's homecoming and the lamps of Diwali

A widely told tradition, especially in northern India, connects Diwali with **Rama's return to Ayodhya with Sita and Lakshmana after exile and the defeat of Ravana**. Lamps welcome their homecoming. [Delhi Tourism recounts the association with Rama's return](https://www.delhitourism.gov.in/dt/festivals/diwali.html).

This is one important telling among several regional traditions. It need not stand as a single explanation for everyone's Diwali.

For this reflection, the image of homecoming raises a gentle question: what makes a home welcoming? Lamps can brighten the entrance. Patience, affection and the willingness to listen shape what someone finds inside.

### A new year does not need one universal story

Gujarati New Year marks a calendar beginning within a living regional tradition. I do not need to attach a single origin story to it to value its greetings, gatherings and fresh intentions. The possibility of beginning again is already close at hand when I make time for another person.

## 4. Everyday example: when the celebration gets busy

Imagine a family preparing for Diwali. Someone is getting ready for the temple, someone is arranging diyas, and someone is packing sweets to take next door. Everyone has a slightly different idea of when to leave. A small delay becomes an irritated remark.

The day can continue from there in several ways. Someone may notice the tension, lower their voice and help with what remains. The plans still matter, but there is room to remember the people carrying them out.

This is an imagined example, not a memory from my family. It is familiar enough to make the reflection practical:

- What happens to my manner when a celebration does not follow my plan?
- Can I notice another person's effort while I am busy with my own?
- What small act would make the next few minutes warmer for everyone?

## 5. A Vedantic lens: what am I asking the celebration to give me?

One thread connects these occasions for me: **how I relate to what I receive and what I do**.

Gold, sweets, lights and time with family can bring real enjoyment. Vedantic inquiry invites me to examine the expectations I place on those experiences. Do I appreciate them as they arrive, or insist that they make me permanently secure, satisfied or appreciated?

On Dhanteras, that question touches acquisition. I can enjoy buying gold when possible and still examine the feeling that one more possession will finally make me complete. The related study note, [Desire, the Self, and Dependence on the World](../chapter-01/10-desire-self-and-worldly-dependence.md), explores this dependence more closely.

On Diwali, the question touches action. Can I participate wholeheartedly without demanding that everyone follow my plan or recognise my effort? In [Bhagavad Gita 2.47](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/2/verse/47/), Krishna directs attention to action, cautions against attachment to its fruits and also rejects attachment to inaction. Applied here, I can prepare, worship and share with care while loosening my demand for a particular response.

The diya offers a further image for reflection: **understanding can reveal something that excitement alone leaves unseen**. I may notice impatience during preparations, comparison while buying, or distraction during a family conversation. Seeing that habit clearly gives me something to work with. The flame is an image for this inquiry; lighting it does not by itself constitute Self-knowledge.

At Gujarati New Year, the question becomes practical. What will I carry into ordinary days? A warmer greeting, a little less hurry when someone speaks, or a regular act of generosity can give continuity to the celebration. This connects with [Vedanta Must Be Practiced](../chapter-01/07-practice-vedanta.md): understanding asks to be lived.

This is a **reflective reading alongside Vedanta**. It does not claim that these festivals were created to teach this particular sequence, or that all Vedantic schools interpret them alike.

## 6. Questions for reflection

Take five to ten quiet minutes. Begin with whichever question feels closest to your experience, and return to the others when you wish.

1. **What do I count as wealth?** Alongside what I hope to buy or earn, what already sustains my life and deserves my gratitude?
2. **What could I see more clearly this Diwali?** Is there one expectation or habit that affects how I treat people during the celebration?
3. **How does my gratitude become action?** What would it look like to carry the attention of temple worship or vehicle pooja into an ordinary day?
4. **How can I make room for someone else?** When I share sweets or spend time with family, what would help me offer my attention as well?
5. **What will I carry into the new year?** Choose one small action that could keep the warmth of these celebrations alive after the festivities end.

## 7. The invitation of these festivals

Gold held in the hand, a diya at home, sweets taken next door and a meal with family: each gives this festive season a familiar shape. I can enjoy those moments and also let them deepen my gratitude.

Worship and family traditions remain meaningful in their own right. Reflection can accompany them, giving me a way to consider how I receive prosperity, share joy and begin again.

**You do not have to transform your whole life before the new year begins.** Choose one expression of care you can continue when the diyas have gone out and the ordinary day returns.
