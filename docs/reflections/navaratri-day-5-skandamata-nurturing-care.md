# Day 5 of Navaratri — Skandamata: Whom Am I Nurturing?

*Part of the [Navaratri series](navaratri.md).*

> **A guided reflection for the fifth day of Navaratri**  
> Explore how Skandamata is worshipped, the traditional account of the mother of Skanda, the meaning of her symbols, and a Vedantic reading of care as a way of seeing the one Self in others. The questions are invitations, not tests.

## 1. How the day is observed

Skandamata (*Skandamātā*) is worshipped as the fifth of the nine forms of Goddess Durga, the Navadurga, on the fifth day of Navaratri. Her name means "mother of Skanda", Skanda being another name of Kartikeya (also called Murugan or Subrahmanya in different regions). She is the form of the Goddess shown as a mother, with her child on her lap.

Customs differ widely, so what follows is a general picture, not a rule:

- **Temple visits and home worship.** Many families pray at home, or visit a temple of the Goddess if it is feasible.
- **Fasting.** Fasting during Navaratri is common, though its form varies by family and region.
- **A common mantra:**

  > ॐ देवी स्कन्दमातायै नमः  
  > *Om Devi Skandamatayai Namah*  
  > "Om, salutations to the Goddess Skandamata."

- **Regional flavours of Navaratri** (festival-wide, not specific to Day 5):
    - In **Gujarat**, *Garba* and *Dandiya Raas* are danced in the evenings through the nine nights.
    - In **West Bengal**, the festival is celebrated as *Durga Puja*, with elaborate community pandals, mostly in the last days.
    - In **Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh**, many homes display *Golu* or *Bommala Koluvu*, a tiered arrangement of dolls.
    - In **Karnataka**, Mysuru's *Dasara* is known for its processions and palace celebrations.

I do not have a fixed family ritual for this day beyond going to the temple when possible. If your family follows a particular practice, that practice comes first. This article is an additional way to reflect, not a replacement.

## 2. The story: the mother of the commander

One common telling connects her to the story of the demon Tarakasura. He is said to have received a boon that only a son of Shiva could defeat him. Shiva was then deep in meditation, and the gods were in distress. Through the union of Shiva and Parvati, Skanda was born, and he grew up to lead the army of the gods and defeat Tarakasura.

In this day's worship, the focus is not on the battle but on the mother. Parvati is remembered as the one who bore, raised and prepared the child who would later do what she herself would not be doing. Because Skanda is the commander of the gods, she is sometimes also called the mother of the commander.

Versions of this story differ between the Puranas, regions and lineages, so please read this as one common telling.

## 3. The form and its symbols

In the traditional description, Skandamata has four arms and is seated on a lotus, which is why she is also called *Padmasana Devi*. A lion is commonly described as her mount. **The tradition gives the form; the interpretations below are suggestions** and may differ between lineages. Published descriptions are not fully uniform, so details such as which hand holds which object vary.

| Feature | Traditional description | A possible meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Child Skanda on her lap** | She holds the infant Skanda in one arm. | Care that holds, protects and prepares someone else to grow. |
| **Two lotuses** | Held in two of her hands. | Purity and grace, and something that grows beautifully while rooted in mud. |
| **Raised hand (abhaya mudra)** | One hand is shown in a gesture of blessing and protection. | Reassurance: "you need not be afraid." |
| **Lotus seat** | She is seated on a lotus. | Stillness and non-attachment, even in the midst of caring for others. |
| **Lion as mount** | Often described as symbolising courage. | Strength in service of the one being cared for; gentleness is not weakness. |
| **Fair complexion** | Commonly described as luminous, white or golden. | Clarity and a pure heart. |

Compare her with Day 4. Kushmanda points to the source from which light arises. Skandamata shows that light being passed on, from one generation or person to the next.

## 4. Everyday example: the teacher who stays after class

Imagine a teacher who notices that one student is quietly struggling. The syllabus does not require anything more. Still, the teacher stays back, explains the lesson again in a different way, and makes sure the student leaves with some confidence.

Years later, the student may not remember the lesson, but remembers how it felt to be noticed. The teacher never sees most of what grows from that moment. **Real nurturing often does its work where the nurturer will not see the result.**

Ask yourself:

- Who has quietly looked after me in a way I did not fully notice at the time?
- Who in my life, a child, a colleague, a parent or a friend, might need some attention from me this week?
- Is my care sometimes mixed with a need to be thanked, or to control how the other person turns out?

## 5. A Vedantic lens: care without possession

The Bhagavad Gita, Chapter 9, verse 17, includes the line:

> पिताहमस्य जगतो माता धाता पितामहः ।

Here Krishna speaks of himself as the father, the mother, the sustainer and the grandfather of the world. In this verse, the tradition says, the Divine is not only a power far away but also the nurturing principle behind everything that sustains life.

Read this way, Skandamata's image suggests two threads:

1. **Care can be a form of seeing.** When I look after someone, I am treating their well-being as something that matters as much as mine. Vedanta points to the one Self present in all beings, and care is one way this can begin to be felt in daily life. See [Vedanta Must Be Practiced](../chapter-01/07-practice-vedanta.md).
2. **Care is easier when it is held loosely.** A mother's love, in this image, prepares the child for a life she will not live for him. Vedanta asks a similar question of our relationships: can I love fully without needing the other person to stay small, or to turn out as I imagined? See [Desire, Self and Worldly Dependence](../chapter-01/10-desire-self-and-worldly-dependence.md).

This is a **reflective reading**, not a claim that every tradition interprets the Goddess in this way. The deeper Vedantic point is understood through careful study with a qualified teacher and not through a single verse or an image.

## 6. Questions for reflection

Set aside five to ten quiet minutes. You may look at an image of Skandamata, offer a prayer, or simply sit. Write a few words for each prompt. "I don't know yet" is a valid answer.

1. **Whom am I nurturing right now?** It may be a child, a parent, a student, a friend, a plant, or even a part of myself.
2. **Who nurtured me?** Whose care helped me become who I am, and have I ever said thank you?
3. **Does my care come with conditions?** Do I quietly expect gratitude, obedience or a certain outcome in return?
4. **Where do I find it hard to let go?** What would it look like to support someone's growth without steering it?
5. **How do I care for myself?** What is one small, kind thing I could do for myself this week, so that I can keep caring for others?

You do not have to answer all five in one sitting. Choose one that feels alive and return to the others later.

## 7. The invitation of Day 5

Skandamata's image is a mother with a child on her lap, and yet she sits calmly on a lotus. Care and steadiness are held together, and the care is not clinging.

Religious worship of the Goddess remains meaningful in its own right. This reflection is one additional way to engage with the day: to notice whom I nurture, who nurtured me, and how lightly I can hold the people I love.

**You do not have to do something big for anyone today.** Noticing one person who needs a little attention, and giving it, is a sincere place to begin.
