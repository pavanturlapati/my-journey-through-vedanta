# Mystery of Origin | From the Known to the Unknown

**Source:** Handwritten opening of *Chapter 2 — World and Human Being*

This page opens Chapter 2. It asks a plain question: if "God" is the answer to the origin of the world, has anything actually been explained? The notes use that question to introduce how Vedanta proceeds, from what is known toward what is not yet known.

## The Origin Question and the Name "God"

<div class="handwritten-note">
① Many questions → Chicken vs Egg; Tree vs Seed; Cause vs Effect. To appease the disturbed mind a name was given to that Causeless Cause. The name was God.

② Some posit a personal God with a form. Others without a form. None realises that God is just another word for origin. That does not solve the fundamental question as to the origin of the world. Nevertheless from time immemorial humanity has passively accepted God as the originator, creator of everything.
</div>

<div class="ai-note">
The examples in the note are all chains in which each item seems to come from the other: the chicken from the egg and the egg from the chicken, the tree from the seed and the seed from the tree. Following causes backwards, the mind looks for something that is itself uncaused, a "Causeless Cause".

The note's point is that giving that starting point a name can calm the mind without answering the question. Whether one imagines a God with a form or without one, the word still stands for "the origin", and what the origin actually is has not yet been explored.

This is the note's argument about <em>explanation</em>. It is not a statement that belief in God is worthless. The next sections show where the notes go from here.
</div>

## Why the Ready Answer Does Not Answer

<div class="handwritten-note">
③ The origin of the world is unknown to the masses. People wish to know the unknown origin. They enquire. They question. The ready answer provided to them is: God is the origin. God created the world. Most people accept it. They are satisfied. But it does not answer the question since God is equally unknown.
</div>

<div class="ai-note">
Here the note is about a ready-made answer. If I ask what the origin of the world is and I am told "God", I have swapped one unknown for another. The answer feels complete, but I know no more than before about either the origin or God.

<strong>Everyday example:</strong> Suppose someone asks why a car will not start and is told, "Because of a fault." The reply sounds like an answer, but unless the fault is identified and understood, nothing has been learned. The word "God" in this note plays the role of "a fault" in the question of the world's origin.

The note does not say the question should be dropped. It says an answer that is as unknown as the question cannot settle it.
</div>

## Knowledge Proceeds from the Known to the Unknown

<div class="handwritten-note">
④ How can one unknown convey the knowledge of another unknown factor? Knowledge of anything can be conveyed only through known factors. Knowledge proceeds from the known to the unknown. It is the fundamental principle of education.
</div>

<div class="ai-note">
This is the rule that holds the whole argument together. Any teaching starts from something the learner already knows. A child learns a new word through words and objects already familiar, and a new mathematical idea is built on earlier ones.

If both the thing to be explained and the thing used to explain it are unknown, no learning can happen. The principle therefore sets a standard: to learn about the unknown, find a starting point that is actually known.
</div>

## Terms We Cannot Conceive

<div class="handwritten-note">
Similarly, the mystery of the unknown can be solved in terms of what is already known to you. God is said to be infinite love, infinite mercy, infinite power etc. But the human intellect cannot conceive infinity.

All these terms are unknown. Such unknown agents cannot solve the mystery of the unknown. The intelligent become conscious of their incapacity and give up their pursuit of God. The ignorant believe that they know God. In their innocence they foist their undigested concepts to the world. They are like the “six blind men who <strong>described</strong> an elephant in a poem by John Godfrey Saxe”.
</div>

<div class="ai-note">
The note applies the same principle to the usual descriptions of God. "Infinite love", "infinite mercy" and "infinite power" use words we know, but the word "infinite" takes them beyond anything the intellect can picture. So the description does not move us from the known to the unknown after all.

Two reactions are described. Some thoughtful people recognise their limits and give up the pursuit. Others feel sure they already know and pass on their own half-digested ideas as if they were the whole truth.

The reference is to John Godfrey Saxe's poem about six blind men who each touch a different part of an elephant, such as the side, the tusk or the trunk. Each is confident that the whole elephant is like his part, and they argue. The comparison suggests that each fixed idea of God can be a real but partial touch that is mistaken for the whole.
</div>

## Vedanta's Approach: Bridging the Gulf

<div class="handwritten-note">
Vedanta gives no fixed names to God and its approach to God is methodical. It systematically bridges the gulf between you and God. The Supreme Godhead lies within you. It is your original nature. Your ignorance veils your Supreme Self. Vedanta gives you the knowledge to discover your Self. Self is your original Being. Ignorance is the disease. The knowledge of Vedanta removes ignorance to restore your Self to yourself.
</div>

<div class="ai-note">
After the problem comes the proposed way forward. The notes say Vedanta does not begin with a fixed name or picture of God. It begins with a method, and the method turns toward the one thing every person has some access to: oneself.

In the language of these notes, the Supreme is not far away but is one's own original nature, covered over by ignorance. The image used is a disease and its cure: ignorance is what is wrong, and knowledge is what removes it. The result is described as "restoring your Self to yourself".

As in Chapter 1, this is a central Vedantic perspective to investigate and test through study and practice, and not simply a belief to accept.
</div>

## Why Knowledge Is Needed, and Where It Stops

<div class="handwritten-note">
Terrestrial knowledge however subtle cannot take you directly to the transcendental Reality. It is not possible to capture God with words, feelings or thoughts. God is infinite. Finite efforts cannot achieve the Infinite. Nevertheless, one cannot reach God without this knowledge. Knowledge can take you only to the subtlest terrestrial experience. Thereafter you will have to experience the Reality subjectively. → Read: Chandra – Shakha – nyaya (Moon suddenly appears to him). So, Vedanta provides all the knowledge necessary to reach your Godhead. It takes you from your present state to the point of perfection, stage of Self-realisation.
</div>

<div class="ai-note">
Two things are held together here. First, words, feelings and thoughts are finite and cannot capture the infinite. Second, the knowledge Vedanta provides is still necessary, because it carries the seeker as far as the subtlest ordinary experience. After that point, the note says, the Reality has to be experienced "subjectively", meaning directly in one's own experience and not only as an idea.

The handwritten arrow "Read: Chandra – Shakha – nyaya" is a pointer for further reading. I understand it to be a traditional maxim, sometimes called the "moon on the branch" illustration, in which a branch near the moon is used to point it out, and the moon is then seen directly. I have not verified the details, so please treat that explanation as provisional until you have read the source.

On a small scale this is how a good teacher works. The teacher can point and explain, but the student has to take the final step of seeing it.
</div>

## Purifying the Mind

<div class="handwritten-note">
Vedantic Knowledge will purify your mind. Make your mind contemplative, meditative. Plunge the purified mind into the silence of meditation. You will then discover your Self within.
</div>

<div class="ai-note">
The role of knowledge here is practical: it prepares the mind. A mind that is less agitated by confusion and wrong ideas is more able to become still and attentive. The note describes meditation as the place where this stillness is used, and the discovery of the Self is said to follow from it.

The order is worth noticing: knowledge first, then a purified mind, then meditation. The study comes before the silence and not in place of it.
</div>

## From the Known World to the Unknown

<div class="handwritten-note">
Therefore, to realize the unknown God you will have to proceed from the known world. Study and analyze the world. Make full use of it to explore the realm of the unknown.
</div>

<div class="ai-note">
This is the conclusion that ties the page back to the chapter title, <em>World and Human Being</em>. If knowledge must proceed from the known to the unknown, then the world, which everyone can observe, is the right starting point. The world is not something to be dismissed on the way to God. It is treated as the working material for inquiry.

In practical terms, this invites careful observation of the world and of oneself, and the questions that come out of that observation are the beginning of the study.
</div>

## Reflection to Carry Forward

<div class="ai-note">

> **If I say "God created the world", what do I actually know that I did not know before, and what could I start to investigate from what I do know?**

A note of nuance: these are the ideas as understood through the handwritten study notes. They are a Vedantic line of reasoning about knowledge and explanation. Other traditions and individuals understand God and creation differently, and this journal is an invitation to study and question, not to settle that for anyone.

</div>

## Check Your Understanding

??? question "1. Why does the note say that calling God the origin does not answer the question of the world's origin?"
    Because God is described as equally unknown. Replacing one unknown (the origin) with another (God) leaves the learner knowing no more than before.

??? question "2. What is the fundamental principle of education in these notes?"
    Knowledge proceeds from the known to the unknown. Anything new can only be explained through something already known to the learner.

??? question "3. Why can't terms like “infinite love” or “infinite power” solve the mystery?"
    Because the human intellect cannot conceive infinity, so these terms remain unknown to us. They cannot serve as the known starting point that learning needs.

??? question "4. What does the elephant-and-blind-men comparison suggest?"
    That people can hold a real but partial idea of God and mistake it for the whole, and then argue about it with others who hold different parts.

??? question "5. According to the notes, what is the role of Vedantic knowledge?"
    It removes ignorance, which is said to veil the Self, and purifies the mind so that it can become contemplative and meditative. It prepares the seeker to experience the Reality directly.

??? question "6. Why does the page end by pointing to the world?"
    Because knowledge must start from what is known, and the world is known. The notes suggest studying and analysing the world to explore what is not yet known.
